## 2a. The early Middle Ages and the deep origin of the North–South divide
- **Slide anchors:** economic/intellectual decline; constant re-organization and
  occupation (Muslim rule in the south, then Normans; north in the Holy Roman
  Empire); medieval castles; a peninsula scattered into many small states.
- **Beyond the slides:**
  - After Rome: **Ostrogoths** (Theodoric); Justinian's devastating **Gothic
    Wars (535–554)** that wrecked Italy; the **Lombards** (from 568 — a northern
    kingdom plus southern duchies); the **Byzantine** exarchate at Ravenna; and
    the **Papal States** (from the mid-8th c.).
  - **Sicily and the south:** an **Arab emirate** (827–~1091) brought an
    agricultural revolution (citrus, sugarcane, cotton, irrigation) and made
    **Palermo** one of the great Mediterranean cities; then the **Normans**
    (Robert Guiscard, Roger II) forged the **Kingdom of Sicily (1130)** — a
    **centralized, top-down feudal monarchy**, later inherited by the
    Hohenstaufen, Angevins, and Aragonese.
  - **The key contrast:** in the **north**, imperial authority was distant and
    weak, so **cities governed themselves**; in the **south**, a strong
    centralized crown ruled from above and cities stayed subordinate. This is the
    institutional fork later scholarship links to the enduring North–South divide
    in trust and civic capacity.
- **Business-environment thread:** the north learns **self-organization,
  cooperation, and local public goods**; the south inherits **centralized,
  extractive** governance. Same peninsula, two trajectories — and institutions
  persist.

## 2b. The Commercial Revolution and the communes (11th–13th c.)
- **Slide anchors:** commercial revolution of the 11th c.; recovering eastern
  trade; cities gain economic weight; the **Lombard League** vs. Barbarossa
  (Legnano, 1176); long-run effects of the commune experience → **social
  capital** (Guiso–Sapienza–Zingales, "Long-Term Persistence").
- **Beyond the slides:**
  - Trade revived (spices, silk, alum via the Levant), turbocharged by the
    **Crusades** (from 1095), which the maritime republics supplied and financed.
    The **maritime republics** — **Venice, Genoa, Pisa, Amalfi** — became
    commercial powers with overseas colonies and trading quarters.
  - **Communes** were sworn associations of citizens who won self-government —
    elected consuls, then a hired outside magistrate (the *podestà*) to curb
    factionalism. They ran **guilds** (*arti*), courts, taxation, and public
    works.
  - The **Lombard League** (1167) united northern cities against Emperor
    Frederick Barbarossa; the cities won at **Legnano (1176)**; the **Peace of
    Constance (1183)** confirmed effective self-rule under nominal imperial
    suzerainty — a landmark of cities bargaining collectively and winning
    autonomy.
  - **Persistence:** the modern finding (GSZ) is that northern towns that were
    **free communes** around 1000–1300 have, *today*, more non-profit
    associations, more blood/organ donation, and less cheating/tax evasion —
    higher "social capital." Civic self-government left a cultural residue that
    still shapes cooperation and business trust.
- **Business-environment thread:** the commune is the **seedbed of Italian
  entrepreneurialism** — self-organization, credit, contract enforcement,
  collective institutions, and trust — the civic DNA that reappears centuries
  later in the industrial districts (Part 4).

## 2c. The Renaissance economy — Italians invent modern business
- **Slide anchors:** independent northern city-states prosper (13th–16th c.) →
  economic rebirth; major **financial innovations** (banking, accounting,
  tradeable bonds); shipbuilding and fleets; wealth + political freedom fund the
  cultural rebirth (Leonardo; Renaissance architecture).
- **Beyond the slides — the heart of the business story:**
  - **Banking and finance:** the **bill of exchange** (*cambium*) moved money
    across Europe and earned implicit interest around the usury ban.
    **Double-entry bookkeeping** (*partita doppia*) was practiced in Genoa,
    Venice, and Florence by the 13th–14th c. and codified in **Luca Pacioli's
    *Summa* (1494)** — the accounting system the world still uses. Florence's
    gold **florin** (from 1252) and Venice's **ducat** became international
    reserve currencies.
  - **The great banks:** Florence's **Bardi, Peruzzi, and Acciaiuoli**
    "super-companies" financed kings — and the **Bardi–Peruzzi collapse
    (1343–46)**, triggered when **Edward III of England defaulted**, is one of
    history's first great sovereign-debt banking crises. The **Medici Bank**
    (founded 1397 by Giovanni di Bicci) built a multi-branch European network,
    ran the papal account, and recycled banking profits into **patronage**
    (Brunelleschi, Botticelli, Michelangelo). The Renaissance was, in part,
    **finance capital financing culture.**
  - **Venice:** the **Arsenal** — a vast state shipyard that at its peak could
    assemble a galley in a day using standardized parts and a moving
    fitting-out process (proto-industrial, proto-assembly-line). Venetian
    **colleganza/commenda** contracts were risk-sharing venture partnerships
    (an investor funds a traveling merchant; they split profit) — arguably
    proto-venture-capital. The state auctioned berths on convoyed galley
    voyages.
  - **Genoa:** the **Casa di San Giorgio (1407)** managed the city's public
    debt and revenues — a precocious quasi-public bank; Genoese financiers then
    bankrolled the **Spanish crown** in the "age of the Genoese" (~1557–1627,
    Braudel). **Marine insurance** emerged in these cities in the 14th century.
  - **Industry:** Florence's **wool** (*Arte della Lana*) and **silk** guilds
    were export powerhouses; Milan made arms and metal goods; Venice, glass
    (Murano). Guilds ensured quality — and, later, rigidity.
- **Business-environment thread:** in the medieval and Renaissance city, Italy
  **invented the infrastructure of capitalism** — double-entry accounting,
  international banking, negotiable instruments, insurance, public debt, and
  venture partnerships. It also shows the first **seeds of decline**: merchants
  turning into **landed rentiers and nobles**, guild rigidity, and dependence on
  lending to spendthrift monarchs.
