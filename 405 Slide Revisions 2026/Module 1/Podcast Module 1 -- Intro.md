## Instructions for the audio hosts (read this first)
This is a short **preview** to get students ready **before** they attend Module 1. **Keep it to 5 minutes or less** — high-level, inviting, and quick. It is a look-ahead, not a summary, and not a lesson.

- **Length is the first constraint, not the last.** 5 minutes is about 700 spoken words. If in doubt, cut. Name an idea, give it one sentence, move on. Do not expand on anything below, do not work through an example, and do not bring in material of your own. About a dozen short exchanges is the whole episode.
- **Two hosts, specific roles.** Host A has done the reading and is prepared. Host B has NOT done the reading and just wants a quick sense of what's coming. Let B open by asking something like "so what are we actually getting into here?" and let A give the fast tour.
- **Open with a line like:** "Here's a preview of what to expect from Module 1."
- **Future tense throughout.** "In Module 1 we'll look at…", "you'll see…". Never speak as if the class already happened.
- **One sentence of intuition per concept.** Brief is not the same as vague: a listener should come away knowing roughly what opportunity cost, sunk cost and marginal analysis mean. But one sentence each is the budget.
- **Audience.** Executives pursuing an MBA at UCLA Anderson — experienced, working managers, light on formal economics. Describe them that way; do NOT call them "Executive MBA students."
- **Name it "Module 1,"** never a "masterclass." Refer to "the class" or "Module 1" — never "the source material" or "this document."
- **Get the one central distinction right.** A change in a good's **own price** moves you **along** its demand curve — a change in *quantity demanded*. Anything else — income, tastes, the weather, the price of a related good — **shifts the whole curve**, a change in *demand*. Never say "the price of diamonds went up, so demand went up."
- **Convey each concept correctly — don't overstate it.** A **sunk cost** is left out of the decision in front of you; that is not the same as "walk away." **Opportunity cost** is the value of the single **next-best** alternative given up, not the sum of every option turned down.
- **Keep the language measured — go easy on "massive."** The hosts tend to reach for extreme words ("massive", "huge", "enormous", "incredible") every other sentence, and it wears out fast. Use them occasionally, where size is the actual point, and let the facts carry the emphasis otherwise.
- Warm, energetic, curious tone. Close by encouraging students to come to class ready to dig in.

## What Module 1 will cover (a trailer — one sentence per idea, no examples worked through)

Module 1 is the foundation of the course. It has two halves: the machinery for thinking about any market, and three principles about costs. Four short videos cover the machinery before class; the class itself applies it and works through the principles.

**Models are maps.** An economic model is a deliberately simplified picture of the world — wrong on purpose, which is exactly what makes it useful. The skill is picking the model that matches the decision in front of you.

**Market definition.** Before you can say anything about competition or pricing, you have to know which products and which places are in your market. The test is simple: if the price of some other product changes, does demand for yours move?

**Demand and supply.** A demand curve slopes down, a supply curve slopes up, and where they cross is the equilibrium — the price at which what buyers want equals what sellers offer. Above it a surplus pushes prices down; below it a shortage pushes them up.

**The distinction to walk in with.** A change in a good's own price moves you *along* its curve. Anything else — income, tastes, the weather, a new technology — *shifts the whole curve*.

**Opportunity cost.** The real cost of a choice is the value of the best alternative you gave up, whether or not you ever write a check for it. Accountants count only the money that changes hands; economists add the rest.

**Sunk cost.** Money already spent that you cannot recover. Nothing you decide today can change it, so it should play no part in the decision in front of you — easy to say, hard to do.

**Marginal analysis.** The question is never "is this worth doing?" but "is the *next* unit worth doing?" Compare the extra benefit against the extra cost, and stop where the two are equal.

The class brings all of this to life with two blocked retail mergers, a heatwave, a celebrity engagement, a war and the price of wheat, the Los Angeles housing market, a house-flipping decision and a supersonic airliner — but the cases themselves are for class, so just say they are coming.

Underneath everything: count every cost that is really a cost, ignore the ones you cannot change, and decide at the margin. Come ready to dig in.
