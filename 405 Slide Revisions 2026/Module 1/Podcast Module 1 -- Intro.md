## Instructions for the audio hosts (read this first)
This is a short **preview** to get students ready **before** they attend Module 1. Keep it to **about 5 minutes** — high-level, inviting, and quick. It is a look-ahead, not a summary.

- **Two hosts, specific roles.** Host A has done the reading and is prepared. Host B has NOT done the reading and just wants a quick, five-minute sense of what's coming. Let B open by asking something like "so what are we actually getting into here?" and let A give the high-level tour. Keep the back-and-forth light and natural.
- **Open with a line like:** "Here's a preview of what to expect from Module 1."
- **Future tense throughout.** "In Module 1 we'll look at…", "you'll see…", "the class will work through…". Never speak as if the class already happened.
- **Build intuition, but hold back the details.** Don't just name the core concepts — give each a brief, plain-language explanation so a listener comes away actually understanding it. What you DO withhold is the worked examples, the specific numbers, and how each case resolves — those are the payoff for class. Name the example *types* (two big retail mergers, a streaming company, a heatwave, a celebrity engagement, a war and the price of wheat, the Los Angeles housing market, a house-flipping decision, a supersonic airliner) as illustrations, but don't work them through.
- **Audience.** Executives pursuing an MBA at UCLA Anderson — experienced, working managers, light on formal economics. Describe them that way; do NOT call them "Executive MBA students."
- **Name it "Module 1,"** never a "masterclass." Refer to "the class" or "Module 1" — never "the source material" or "this document."
- **Get the one central distinction right.** A change in a good's **own price** moves you **along** its demand curve — that is a change in *quantity demanded*. Anything else — income, tastes, the weather, the price of a related good — **shifts the whole curve**, which is a change in *demand*. Say it plainly and don't blur it. Never say something like "the price of diamonds went up, so demand went up."
- **Convey each concept correctly — don't overstate it.**
  - A **sunk cost** should be left out of the decision in front of you. That is not the same as "walk away." The rule is that money you can't get back is irrelevant now; you decide on future costs and benefits alone, and sometimes that means continuing.
  - **Opportunity cost** is the value of the single **next-best** alternative you gave up — not the sum of every option you turned down.
  - **Market definition** is a matter of degree. When the class asks whether one kind of store competes with another, the answer is usually that some substitution happens but not enough to matter. Don't reduce it to "they're not competitors."
- You may state the one unifying idea in plain, intuitive terms (weigh a little more benefit against a little more cost, and do more until they balance), but keep it light — the class develops it fully.
- **Keep the language measured — go easy on "massive."** The hosts tend to
  reach for extreme words ("massive", "huge", "enormous", "incredible",
  "game-changing") every other sentence, and it wears out fast. Those words
  are not banned, but use them **occasionally** — a handful of times across
  the whole episode, where the size of something is the actual point. The
  rest of the time say it plainly and let the fact carry the emphasis: "the
  largest supermarket merger ever proposed" already tells the listener how
  big it is without a "massive" in front of it.
- Warm, energetic, curious tone. Close by encouraging students to come to class ready to dig in.

## What Module 1 will cover (preview — build intuition, but no worked examples or numbers)

Module 1 is the foundation of the whole course. It does two things: it hands you the basic machinery for thinking about any market — supply, demand, and the price where they meet — and then it lays down three principles about **costs** that separate good business decisions from bad ones. Four short videos cover the market machinery before class, and the class itself is spent applying it and working through the three principles together.

The module opens with what an economic model actually is. A model is a deliberately simplified picture of the world: you state some assumptions, follow the logic, and get a conclusion. The class will use the analogy of a **map**. A map is wrong on purpose — it leaves almost everything out — and that is exactly why it is useful. The skill you'll build all quarter is choosing the model that matches the decision in front of you, the way you'd choose a highway map for a road trip and a street map for a delivery route.

Then comes **market definition**, and it is far less obvious than it sounds. Before you can say anything about competition, pricing, or market share, you have to decide which products and which places belong in the same market as yours. The practical test is simple to state: if the price of some other product changes, does demand for yours move? If it barely does, that product is in a different market. This question is not academic — it decides antitrust cases worth billions, and the class will look at two recent mergers that were blocked, and one streaming company where reasonable people still disagree about where the boundary lies.

Next, the workhorse of the entire course: **demand and supply**. A demand curve slopes downward — buyers want more as the price falls — and a supply curve slopes upward, because higher prices make it worth producing more. Where they cross is the **equilibrium**: the price at which the amount buyers want equals the amount sellers offer. Above it there is a surplus and prices get bid down; below it there is a shortage and prices get bid up. That is the market mechanism, and once you can draw it you can reason about almost any market.

The single most important distinction in this part — and the one students most often get wrong — is between a **movement along** a curve and a **shift of** the curve. A change in the good's own price moves you along the curve. Anything else — income, tastes, the weather, a technology, the price of a related good — moves the entire curve to a new position. The class will practise this on a heatwave, on a celebrity engagement, on the boom in demand for AI chips, on a war that cut off a major wheat exporter, and on the Los Angeles housing market, where the interesting twist is that both curves moved at once.

Then the module turns to costs, and to the three principles that make up the economic way of thinking.

The first is that **economic costs include opportunity costs**. The real cost of any choice is the value of the best alternative you gave up, whether or not you ever write a check for it. Accountants count only the money that actually changes hands; economists add the implicit costs too, and the gap between those two numbers is where a great many bad decisions live. The class will run this on the full cost of an MBA, on a house-flipping opportunity, and on the cost of raising a child.

The second is that you should **ignore sunk costs**. A sunk cost is money already spent that you cannot recover. Because nothing you decide today can change it, it should play no part in the decision in front of you — you optimize forward from here. This is easy to say and genuinely hard to do, and the class will look at a famous aviation program to see how hard.

The third is to **use cost-benefit and marginal analysis**. The question is almost never "is this activity worth doing?" It is "is the *next* unit of it worth doing?" Compare the extra benefit of one more unit against the extra cost of that one more unit, and keep going while the benefit is larger. Stop where the two are equal. Economists write that as MB equals MC, and it is the single most reused idea in this course.

Listen for that habit of mind running underneath all of it: count every cost that is really a cost, ignore the ones you cannot change, and decide at the margin. Come ready to dig in.
