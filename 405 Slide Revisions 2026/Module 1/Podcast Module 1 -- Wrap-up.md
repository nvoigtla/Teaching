## Part 1 — The economic way of thinking

### Models are maps

Module 1 opened with what an economic model actually is: a deliberately simplified framework. You state assumptions, follow the logic, and reach a conclusion. The class used the line usually attributed to the statistician George Box — all models are wrong, but some are useful — and then made it concrete with maps. If you are driving from Los Angeles to San Francisco, you want a map showing the major routes and nothing else. If you are getting around Los Angeles, you want every street. If you are delivering packages in a neighbourhood, you need to see the boundaries between individual houses. A more detailed map is more accurate and less useful. The skill is picking the model that matches the decision.

The class also introduced **homo economicus** — the mythical creature economic models are built on, who is completely selfish, completely rational and completely informed. Real people are none of those things, and the class said so plainly. The point was not that the assumption is true. The point is that a simplification you understand lets you reason quickly and get most decisions right, and you can then add back the nuance where it matters.

There was a nice aside about foxes and hedgehogs, from the old line that the fox knows many things but the hedgehog knows one big thing. Foxes have a different strategy for every problem and are comfortable with nuance. Hedgehogs reduce everything to one organizing principle — quick, decisive, and occasionally wrong. Economists are hedgehogs. In your own industry you are the fox, and the class is really about combining the two.

## Part 2 — Markets: where does your market end?

### Market definition

Before you can say anything about competition or pricing, you have to know which products and which places are in your market. The class gave a practical test for the product boundary: if the price of some other product changes, does demand for yours move? If it does, that product is in your market. If it barely does, it isn't. The geographic boundary varies enormously by product — a coffee shop competes with the next street, a gas station with the next few miles, gold with the entire world.

This is not an academic exercise. It decides antitrust cases.

### Tapestry–Capri: the firms' own documents drew the boundary

In August 2023 Tapestry, which owns Coach and Kate Spade, agreed to buy Capri, which owns Michael Kors and Versace, for about 8.5 billion dollars. Everything then turned on one question: what is the market? The FTC said it was **accessible luxury** handbags — roughly a hundred dollars up to just under a thousand — where these brands sit almost on top of each other. The companies said the market was all handbags, from fast fashion to Hermès, where their combined share looks small and new entry is easy.

The evidence settled it, and this is the part worth remembering. The same quantity — the combined share of accessible-luxury handbags — was computed three ways: about **59 percent** by the FTC's economist using largely third-party data, **77 percent** in Capri's own internal documents, and **83 percent** from Tapestry's own internal data. Those last two came from ordinary-course business documents the companies had to hand over in the merger review — not leaks. Their own people had been describing exactly the market their lawyers were now arguing did not exist. "Accessible luxury" had in fact been the firms' own term, used freely in their filings and investor presentations, until the FTC sued. In October 2024 the court sided with the FTC and blocked the deal, and the merger was abandoned the following month.

### Kroger–Albertsons: is a "Costco run" a substitute?

In October 2022 Kroger agreed to buy Albertsons for **24.6 billion dollars**, the largest supermarket merger ever proposed in the United States. The companies argued they were small next to Walmart, Amazon and Costco. The FTC argued the market was supermarkets, assessed city by city — and in many of those cities the number-one chain was buying the number two.

The court drew the line at supermarkets. Club stores, limited-assortment chains like Aldi and Trader Joe's, dollar and convenience stores and online-only sellers all fell outside. The reasoning was memorable: a monthly trip to Costco to stock up is not a reasonable substitute for a weekly one-stop visit to a supermarket. In December 2024 a federal court in Oregon and a Washington state court both blocked the deal. The lesson the class drew: **some substitution is not the same as enough substitution.**

### Netflix — where students disagreed

The class also asked where Netflix's market ends. Online streaming? All film and television, cinemas included? Everything competing for your evening? Each answer implies a different set of competitors and a different picture of market power, and the class did not settle on one. That was the point.

## Part 3 — Demand, supply, and equilibrium

### The two curves

A **demand curve** is the relationship between the quantity buyers are willing to buy and the price — holding everything else constant. It slopes downward: cheaper, and more people buy. A **supply curve** is the same relationship on the seller's side and slopes upward, because producing more usually costs more per unit, so a higher price is needed to make it worth doing.

The phrase "holding everything else constant" — *ceteris paribus* — is doing real work there. It means income, tastes, the prices of related goods, even the weather are all frozen while we vary the one price.

### Movement along versus shift of the curve

This is the distinction the whole module turns on, and it is the one most people get wrong. A change in the good's **own price** moves you **along** the curve; that is a change in *quantity demanded*. A change in **anything else** moves the **entire curve**; that is a change in *demand*. Same picture, two completely different events.

The class ran this through several examples:

- **AI and computer chips.** Nothing about the price of a chip changed. What changed is how badly firms want them, because training and running AI models takes enormous numbers of specialised processors. So the whole demand curve moved out.
- **A Los Angeles heatwave and air conditioners.** A heatwave is a non-price factor. At every price, more people want an air conditioner, so the demand curve shifted right.
- **Taylor Swift's engagement and diamonds.** When she announced her engagement to Travis Kelce in August 2025 and posted a picture of the ring, searches for diamond rings surged and jewellery retailers' shares moved the next day. Again a non-price factor, again a rightward shift of demand.

### Equilibrium and the market mechanism

Where the two curves cross is the **equilibrium** — the price at which the quantity supplied equals the quantity demanded. The class showed why it is stable. Above that price, sellers offer more than buyers want: excess supply, a surplus, and sellers cut prices to move it. Below it, buyers want more than sellers offer: excess demand, a shortage, and buyers bid the price up. Either way the market is pushed back to the crossing point. Adam Smith's invisible hand, drawn as two lines.

Then the shifts. If demand shifts right, the market clears at a **higher** price and a **larger** quantity. If supply shifts right, it clears at a **lower** price and a larger quantity. And if both curves shift at once, only one of the two directions is pinned down — the other depends on which shift is bigger. That last point is worth holding on to, because it is the answer to a lot of real-world puzzles.

### Applying it: three cases

**Wheat and the war in Ukraine.** When the war began, a major exporter was cut off. Supply shifted left and the price jumped. When the Black Sea grain agreement reopened the export route, supply shifted back out and the price fell. Same market, two shocks, both on the supply side.

**Los Angeles housing, 2021 to 2025.** Inflation-adjusted home prices fell only about **5 percent**, while the number of sales fell roughly **40 percent**. A tiny price move next to a huge quantity move is a clue that this cannot be one curve moving on its own. Demand shifted left — higher mortgage rates, people leaving. But supply shifted left too, because owners holding cheap old mortgages were locked in and simply did not list. Two leftward shifts: quantity collapses, price barely budges.

**Shortages when disasters loom.** Before a storm or during the pandemic, shelves empty out. Standard supply and demand says the price should rise until the market clears — so why a shortage instead? Part of the answer is that sellers worry about being seen as unfair, and that constraint is itself an economic force worth taking seriously.

The class also gave a recipe worth reusing: name the shock, decide whether it hits demand or supply, then draw both curves before and after. Most mistakes come from skipping the middle step.

## Part 4 — The three cost principles

### Principle 1: economic costs include opportunity costs

The **opportunity cost** of a choice is the value of the **next-best** alternative you gave up. It is usually implicit — you never write a check for it — which is exactly why it gets missed. Full economic cost is the explicit cost plus the implicit one. Accountants see only the explicit part.

**The full cost of an MBA.** Tuition is the explicit cost. For most people in the room, the larger number is the income and the career progress given up while studying. The class left an open question hanging: does a remote or part-time option change that calculation?

**Flip a house or work?** This is the example to remember, and it needs its numbers. You buy a house for **500k**, put in another **100k** of remodelling plus a full year of your own time, and expect to sell for **700k**. On paper: 700 minus 600 is a **100k gain**. But the year of your own time was not free — you turned down a consulting job paying **150k** that you could not do at the same time. Count that implicit cost and the economic profit is **minus 50k**. Same facts, opposite decision. And the class pushed one step further: the 500k of capital could itself have been invested elsewhere and earned a return, which is another opportunity cost — whether you count it depends on where the money came from.

**The cost of raising a child.** The USDA's categories — housing, food, transport, clothing, education and child care, health — add up to roughly a quarter of a million dollars to age eighteen. And that leaves out the biggest items. One economist quoted in the coverage put the real figure for a moderate-income family closer to **900,000 dollars to age 22** once you include forgone income, college for those who attend, and the return on money that was never invested. The class then showed the earnings paths of men and women around the birth of a first child, in Denmark and in the United States. The shape is strikingly similar across countries, and every bit of those forgone earnings is an opportunity cost.

### Principle 2: ignore sunk costs

A **sunk cost** is one that has been paid and cannot be recovered. Because nothing you decide now can change it, it should play no part in the decision in front of you. The difficulty is psychological, not analytical — walking away feels like admitting waste.

Everyday versions are easy to spot once you look: finishing dessert because the dinner was a fixed price, going to the gym because you already paid for the year, skiing in bad weather because the lift ticket is non-refundable. Firms do the same thing with license fees and advertising already spent.

**Concorde** is the classic case, and it is worth telling carefully. It was a genuine engineering triumph — New York to London in under three hours — and a source of national pride for Air France and British Airways. It never recovered its development cost, and in the end it did not even cover its operating costs. That last fact is what made the decision clear: once the money you are still spending exceeds what you are taking in, the past spending is irrelevant and you stop. The national pride is precisely what made stopping so hard.

The takeaway the class landed on: decide looking forward. You cannot change the past, so optimize from here. Sunk costs usually show up because something unforeseen changed the right answer — and when that happens, you re-optimize as though the money had never been spent.

### Principle 3: use cost-benefit and marginal analysis

The objective is to maximize **net benefit** — total benefit minus total cost. To find it you compare at the **margin**: the extra benefit from one more unit against the extra cost of that one more unit, opportunity costs included. Take the extra unit while marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost. Stop where the two are equal. MB equals MC.

The class made it concrete with hours of exercise. The first hour carries a large net benefit. The second, less. By the fourth, the extra benefit and the extra cost are equal — you are indifferent. A fifth hour would cost more than it is worth. So four is the optimum. Notice what was never asked: whether exercise is good. Only whether the *next* hour is.

Drawn continuously — marginal benefit sloping down, marginal cost sloping up, the optimum where they cross — that picture will come back in every module of this course.

## Wrapping up

Module 1 gave the framework and the discipline. The framework is markets, demand and supply, and the equilibrium where they meet. The discipline is three principles: economists count implicit costs that accountants do not; sunk costs get ignored; and decisions are made at the margin. Everything that follows in the course builds on those.
