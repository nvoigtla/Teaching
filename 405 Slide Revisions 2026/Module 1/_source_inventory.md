## S8 (slide8.xml) [ANIM] — ECONOMIC MODELS
- [sp] Models are theoretical frameworks that help us understand the world around us. / “All models are wrong, but some are useful” (George Box – Famous Statistician). / Models are like maps: they simplify the complex world to help you navigate it. / Find a model that matches your needs.
- [sp] 8
- NOTES: Architects need their rulers. Doctors need their stethoscopes. Economists need their models.
What are economic models? They are theoretical frameworks that help us understand the world around us. You state some assumptions, and through a series of logical steps you reach some conclusions. Sometimes these models are mathematical. Sometimes they come in the form of charts.
Models are always wrong, but sometimes they are useful.
In a sense, economic models are like maps. Maps provide a simplified version of the real world to help us navigate it.
You may want to use a simple map, or a more complicated map, depending on your needs.

## S10 (slide10.xml) [ANIM] — HOMO ECONOMICUS pics:3
- [sp] Homo Economicus (seeks to maximize utility or happiness) / 100% selfish / 100% rational / 100% informed
- [sp] Real Human / Some are altruistic / Some make stupid decisions / Some are poorly informed
- [sp] Podcast: Who is Homo Economicus?
- [sp] 10
- NOTES: In this course, I will teach you some economic models that are remarkably wrong, but surprisingly useful. We will abstract away from a lot of the nuance in the real world, to focus on what matters the most for the decision at hand. So, how inaccurate can these economic models be?
Well, economic models are based on a mythical creature called homo economicus. Homo economicus are 100% Selfish: they don’t care how their actions will affect others. Homo economicus are 100% Rational: everyone is super smart and can make optimal decisions in a blink of an eye. Homo economicus are 100% Informed: everyone knows all the relevant information, including prices, wages, taxes, and so on.
We know real people are not like that. Some people are altruistic. Some people make stupid decisions – at least I do. Some people are poorly informed – the world is complicated, so it would be impossible to stay informed about everything all the time.
So, even though we know these models are wrong, they are still useful. They simplify our thinking, to help us make decisions quickly. You may wonder: why do we even need the homo economicus in the first place? Why do we need to simplify the world so much?

## S11 (slide11.xml) [ANIM] — ECONOMISTS AS HEDGEHOGS pics:2
- [sp] “The fox knows many things, but the hedgehog knows one big thing” (Greek aphorism). / Foxes have different strategies for different problems. They are comfortable with nuance. / Hedgehogs, on the other hand, focus on the big picture. They reduce every problem to one organizing principle.
- [sp] 11
- NOTES: The answer is simple: economists are like hedgehogs. Let me explain. Someone once said:
“The fox knows many things, but the hedgehog knows one big thing.” If you think these are the lyrics of a new Lady Gaga song, that’s not it. These words were attributed to the Ancient Greek poet Archilochus, and popularized by the philosopher Isaiah Berlin. This allegory suggests that there are two types of people in the world: the foxes, and the hedgehogs.
On the one hand, foxes have different strategies for different problems. They love nuance, and want to spend all the time in the world to navigate the nitty-gritty of the problem at hand.
Hedgehogs, on the other hand, want to reduce every problem to one organizing principle. They have one universal tool that they will attempt to use no matter what the problem is. This has a clear disadvantage: if you don’t pay attention to nuance, you will make mistakes. However, being a Hedgehog has its advantages too: you can make decisions quickly and with little effort, almost instinctively.

- [sp] The Economic Way of Thinking · Discussion
- [sp] Discussion: WSJ Barro's Article
- [sp] Management 405 · The Economic Way of Thinking
- [sp] 16
- [sp] Maximizing long term profits: is this the right/ethical goal?
- [sp] Would Bill Gates create more social value by running Microsoft or by giving away his money through the Gates Foundation?
- [sp] Class discussion
- NOTES: Question for debate: Did Bill Gates create more social value by running Microsoft and supplying Windows to the market or by giving away his billions fighting AIDS in Africa? Probably the former. From Robert Barro:
“Here is a sketch of a simple model of Microsoft's social value. The market value of the company's stock recently hit $287 billion. In 2006, its revenue was $44 billion, with earnings of $13 billion. This money was generated by creating something consumers value. Only Microsoft's competitors could believe that this much market value, revenue and earnings would have been created by delivering products that have little value to society.
Suppose that a copy of a new version of Windows sells for $50 (and is typically charged as part of the price of a personal computer). Microsoft's revenue from Windows would then equal $50 multiplied by the number of copies consumers snap up. Microsoft's earnings are the revenue less production and development expenses. But that's not the social value. That comes from the increase in productivity created when businesses and households use the software. The social benefit equals the value of the extra product, less the total paid for the software. Almost by definition, the benefit has to be positive. Otherwise, why would consumers willingly pay for Windows?
A conservative estimate, in a model where software serves as a new variety of productive input, is that the social benefit of Microsoft's software is at least the $44 billion Microsoft pulls in each year. When capitalized with the same ratio (22) that the market applies to earnings, this flow corresponds to a valuation of $970 billion. Thus, through Microsoft's future operations, Mr. Gates is creating a benefit to the rest of society of about one trillion dollars -- or more than 10 times his planned donations. And this counts only the likely future benefits, giving no weight to the past.” WSJ June 19, 2007.
However, one might also take into account that we as a society might value more money given to the poor (in terms of charity) compared to money given to the well-off (in terms of happiness from using Windows net of the price of license). To complicate the discussion even more, Windows might also help people doing the humanitarian projects, which adds to the value of Bill Gates as an entrepreneur.
We might also want to figure out what is the cost-efficiency of charitable giving: how much value does Bill Gates generate for each additional dollar he gives to charity. To do so, we can look at estimates by GiveWell.org, an NGO using randomized controlled trials to figure out the best ways of spending charitable money.

## S20 (slide20.xml) [ANIM] — (no title placeholder)
- [sp] The Economic Way of Thinking · Opportunity Costs
- [sp] Economic Costs include Opportunity Costs
- [sp] Management 405 · The Economic Way of Thinking
- [sp] 20
- [sp] The opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative sacrificed / >What you give up to get something / The opportunity cost is not always monetary. It is often implicit rather than explicit / >Explicit cost: actual payment / >Implicit cost: forgo an alternative action / Full economic cost = implicit cost + explicit cost
- NOTES: The opportunity cost is the value of the next best opportunity foregone.
E.g. 1: spend 1) $20 on a shirt, 2) $20 on a dinner or 3) $20 on a Taylor Swift album. In all three cases you spend $20 so that is not part of the relevant costs. The real cost is you have to forego the next best alternative. i.e. by choosing 1, you forego 2. It had better be that the benefit of choosing 1 exceeds the benefit of choosing 2 (i.e. the benefit of 1 exceeds the opportunity cost of 1). Cost-benefit analysis in a world of tradeoffs requires a consideration of alternatives. Cost is relative to alternatives.
E.g. 2: What is the opportunity cost of using excess factory capacity to supply specialty orders? If the capacity would otherwise go unused, the opportunity cost is zero (or small – the capacity would be committed in the unlikely event that a regular order would arrive unexpectedly).
E.g. 3: As mayor of Santa Monica you have to decide whether to turn a patch of land owned by the city into a parking lot. What is the opportunity cost of doing so? The land could be sold on the open market at its market price. The opportunity cost is the market price of the land, or the value of some alternative municipal value, whichever is greatest (in general the market price reflects well the value of alternatives). In this case the cost is implicit, but if the city did not own the land, it would have to buy it, and the cost would be explicit.

On paper the total is 175 million against 150 million of revenue, so it looks like a 25 million dollar loss, and the instinct is to shelve it. But that is the sunk-cost trap. The 140 million is already gone whether you release or not. The only number that matters now is the 35 million it takes to release against the 150 million it earns. So you release every single time. The rule at the bottom says it cleanly, ignore sunk costs, and go ahead whenever the extra revenue beats the extra cost.

- [sp] Market equilibrium: where supply and demand intersect, at price P₀ and quantity Q₀ / At a higher price P₁, a surplus develops, so the price falls / At a lower price P₂, there is excess demand, so the price is bid up
- [sp] Price ($)

- [sp] P₀
- [sp] Q₀
- [sp] P₁
- [sp] Surplus

- [sp] Excess demand
- NOTES: The point where they intersect is called market equilibrium, the price at which the quantity supplied equals the quantity demanded.
To see why market equilibrium is stable, let’s go through what happens when the price deviates from the equilibrium condition:
Suppose the price is higher than P_0; What happens at this price? Producers will start entering the market, wanting to sell at this high price, but they won’t all be able to find willing buyers. Then the quantity supplied will be greater than the quantity demanded. The excess quantity supplied is known as surplus. To eliminate the surplus, producers need to attract more buyers and to do so, they have to lower their prices. As price falls, quantity demanded increases, and the quantity supplied falls until the market reaches equilibrium.
Suppose the price is lower than P_0; then the quantity demanded will exceed the quantity supplied and shortage will develop; To eliminate this shortage, buyers who cannot find the good available for sale will bid up the price and enterprising producers will be more than willing to raise their prices. As price rises, quantity demanded falls, quantity supplied rises, until we reach equilibrium.
How does the market reach equilibrium in practice? Adam Smith’s invisible hand.

- NOTES: The point where they intersect is called market equilibrium, the price at which the quantity supplied equals the quantity demanded.
To see why market equilibrium is stable, let’s go through what happens when the price deviates from the equilibrium condition:
Suppose the price is higher than P_0; What happens at this price? Producers will start entering the market, wanting to sell at this high price, but they won’t all be able to find willing buyers. Then the quantity supplied will be greater than the quantity demanded. The excess quantity supplied is known as surplus. To eliminate the surplus, producers need to attract more buyers and to do so, they have to lower their prices. As price falls, quantity demanded increases, and the quantity supplied falls until the market reaches equilibrium.
Suppose the price is lower than P_0; then the quantity demanded will exceed the quantity supplied and shortage will develop; To eliminate this shortage, buyers who cannot find the good available for sale will bid up the price and enterprising producers will be more than willing to raise their prices. As price rises, quantity demanded falls, quantity supplied rises, until we reach equilibrium.
How does the market reach equilibrium in practice? Adam Smith’s invisible hand.
