## What Module 2 will cover (preview — build intuition, but no worked examples or numbers)

Module 2 is about one side of the market: **demand**. The whole module works toward turning a vague fact — customers buy less when prices rise — into a number you can actually use when you set a price.

It starts with **why the demand curve slopes down**, for two separate reasons. At a lower price, more people are willing to buy at all. And each individual customer buys more, because of **diminishing marginal personal value**: the first slice of pizza is worth a lot to you, the second less, the third less again. Out of that comes the consumer's version of the rule from Module 1 — keep buying while the value of the next unit is above what it costs you.

Then the module's central tool: **elasticity**. Elasticity is responsiveness expressed as a ratio of two percentage changes — how much quantity moves, in percent, when something else moves by one percent. Because it is a ratio of percentages it carries **no units**, so the numbers are comparable across products, currencies and countries. Three versions come up. **Own-price elasticity** measures how quantity responds to the product's own price; it is always negative, and demand is called *elastic* when quantity reacts more than proportionally and *inelastic* when it reacts less. **Income elasticity** measures how demand responds to customers' income, and its sign classifies the good: positive for a *normal* good, above one for a *luxury*, negative for an *inferior* good — which does not mean low quality, only that demand rises when incomes fall. **Cross-price elasticity** measures how demand for your product responds to another product's price: positive means *substitutes*, negative means *complements*.

You will also see two ways to compute an elasticity — an approximation from two observed price-and-quantity points, and an exact figure when you know the whole demand curve.

The payoff is what elasticity does for **revenue**. Whether raising your price raises or lowers total revenue depends entirely on which category you are in, and the class draws a sharp conclusion about where a firm should never be pricing. Closely related is **marginal revenue**, the revenue from selling one more unit, which turns out to be lower than the price — for a reason worth puzzling over in advance.

The module closes with **where these numbers come from**: price experiments and regression on real data, plus a caution about why a correlation in ordinary sales data is not causation.

The examples will do the teaching — a streaming service's price increases, a lottery ticket price change, a ride-hailing company's estimated demand curve, two large retailers heading in opposite directions in a recession, the cereal aisle, a weight-loss drug, a fast-food value menu, and an airline pricing experiment. Come ready to work through the numbers.
