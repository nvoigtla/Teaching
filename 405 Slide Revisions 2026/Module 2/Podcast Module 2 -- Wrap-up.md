- **The minus sign.** Own-price elasticity is **always negative**, because price up means quantity down. - **Marginal revenue is below the price.** A firm facing a downward-sloping demand curve has to cut the price to sell one more unit — and it cuts it on **every** unit, not only the last one. The revenue lost on all the other units is why marginal revenue sits below the price.

## Part 1 — Where the demand curve comes from

### Two reasons the curve slopes down

The class started with the law of demand and the assumption people forget: *ceteris paribus*, everything else held fixed. Only under that condition does a lower price mean a higher quantity — so if volume rose last quarter and you also cut the price, you cannot credit the price cut with all of it unless nothing else moved.

Then two separate engines behind the downward slope. First, at a lower price **more people buy at all** — customers who were priced out come in. Second, **each existing customer buys more**.

### Diminishing marginal personal value

The second engine rests on an idea worth keeping. Ask what you would pay for one slice of pizza when you are hungry — not what it costs, but what it is worth to you. That maximum willingness to pay is your **marginal personal value**. You would pay a lot for the first slice, less for the second, less again for the third, and essentially nothing for the millionth. Plot those values and you have drawn your own demand curve.

Bill Gates made the same point about money: asked what it felt like to be a billionaire, he said he could understand wanting a million dollars, but beyond that, "it's the same hamburger." The class backed it with cross-country evidence on income and life satisfaction, which climbs steeply from low incomes and then flattens.

### The consumer's rule, and market demand

That gives the consumer's version of the rule from Module 1: keep buying while the value of the next unit is above what it costs you, and stop where the two meet. The class illustrated it with how many movies to watch in a week, where the cost of one more rises as you go — you give up your best alternative uses of the time first. Read that picture the other way and the value curve **is** the demand curve.

Market demand is then the **horizontal sum** of individual demands: at each price, add up quantities, not prices. Two consequences worth remembering — market demand is flatter than any one person's, and it shifts when the number of customers changes even if nobody's individual demand has moved.

### What shifts demand, and two twists

A change in the good's own price moves you **along** the curve; everything else **shifts** it — income, the prices of related goods, tastes, expectations, the number of buyers.

Two twists the class enjoyed. The **snob effect**: for some products, high demand from other people reduces the appeal. Ferrari caps production well below demand — you have to be invited to buy its top models — and Hermès does the same with the Birkin bag. Exclusivity is part of what customers are buying. The opposite case is **positive network effects**, where a product is worth more to you precisely because others use it. Then switching gets harder, markets tip once one side pulls ahead, and the end state is often winner-take-all — as with two nearly identical social networks around 2007, where the one with the larger network took the market.

## Part 2 — Elasticity: one number for "how responsive?"

### The definition, and why it has no units

Elasticity is responsiveness: the percentage change in one variable divided by the percentage change in another. Because it is a ratio of percentages, it carries **no units** — dollars or euros, gallons or litres, it makes no difference. That is what makes elasticities comparable across products and across countries.

The class motivated it with a streaming service that has raised its standard US plan price repeatedly, from around eight dollars to around eighteen. Every one of those increases faced two questions: how many subscribers will we lose, and will revenue go up or down? Those are different questions, and elasticity is the single number that links them.

Three versions, all with the same structure — the percentage change in quantity demanded, divided by the percentage change in whatever is driving it.

### Own-price elasticity

The percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in the good's own price. By the law of demand the sign is always negative. Three categories, defined on the absolute value: **inelastic** between −1 and 0, **unit-elastic** at exactly −1, **elastic** below −1.

The worked case was water. A consulting firm estimated the price elasticity of demand for water in Los Angeles at −0.4. The Department of Water and Power wanted households to use 10% less, and price was its only lever — so how big does the increase have to be? Because demand is inelastic, the price has to move a lot to move quantity a little: a **25% price increase**. That is the whole point of the number.

The other direction: a yoga studio cut its price by 17% and the number of booked lessons doubled — a 100% increase in quantity, implying an elasticity of about −6. Firmly elastic.

### Two ways to compute it

**Method 1** is for when you have two observed price-and-quantity points. Take both percentage changes relative to the initial point and divide. It only **approximates** the percentage changes, and the approximation gets worse the larger the price change, because the answer depends on which point you call the initial one.

The example was the lottery. On 8 April 2025 Mega Millions raised its ticket price from $2 to $5 — a 150% increase, and only the second price change in the game's history. Researchers collected the sales data: in New York, tickets sold per drawing fell from about 1.9 million to about 560,000, roughly a 70% drop. That gives an implied elasticity of about **−0.47** — well inside the inelastic range, which is exactly why revenue rose even though the number of tickets sold collapsed.

**Method 2**, the point elasticity, is for when you know the whole demand curve. Then no approximation is needed: the elasticity is the slope of the demand curve — the derivative of quantity with respect to price — multiplied by price over quantity. The class worked a simple linear case and got an elasticity of −0.25 at the chosen price, which is inelastic, and set up the next point.

### Elasticity changes along the curve — and the insight that follows

For a straight-line demand curve the **slope** is constant but the **elasticity** is not, because price and quantity both change as you move along it. Demand is elastic in the upper-left portion, at high prices and low quantities, and inelastic in the lower-right.

That leads to the result worth carrying out of the module: **a firm should not operate on the inelastic part of its own demand curve.** The intuition is short. Where demand is inelastic, raising the price loses relatively little quantity, so revenue goes up. Selling less at the same time lowers variable costs. Revenue up and costs down means profit up — so a firm sitting there is leaving money on the table.

The example was ride-hailing: a 2016 study estimated Uber's elasticity at about −0.4, solidly inelastic, and the company subsequently raised prices. These are not textbook numbers — firms really do estimate their own demand curves, and the estimate changes the pricing decision.

### What determines elasticity — and whose elasticity it is

Mostly **substitutes**: how many there are, how close they are, and how willing customers are to switch. Time horizon matters too, since customers find alternatives given time.

The distinction that matters most for pricing is **market versus firm elasticity**. Demand for gasoline overall is quite inelastic — drivers still have to fill up when prices rise. But demand at one particular gas station is highly elastic: raise the price while the station across the intersection doesn't, and customers simply drive over there. A firm's demand is more elastic when the market elasticity is high, when its share is small, and when competitors don't match its price.

### Income elasticity

The percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in income. Unlike own-price elasticity, the **sign is not fixed**, and the sign is what classifies the good. Positive means a **normal** good; above one means a **luxury**, where demand grows faster than income; negative means an **inferior** good, where demand rises when incomes fall.

The worked case: average US income rises 2%, and demand for a particular electric vehicle model rises 5% — an income elasticity of 2.5. Positive, so a normal good, and above one, so a luxury. Which also means a recession hits that product harder than average.

Why the classification pays off: in 2007 one large discount retailer and one large mid-market retailer had very different stock prices. The US economy entered recession in December 2007 and incomes fell. The discounter sells goods closer to the inferior end, so lower incomes **raised** its sales; the mid-market retailer sells normal goods, so lower incomes reduced its demand. The two stock prices went in opposite directions.

A current example of an inferior good is online secondhand clothing: when consumers get worried and incomes tighten, demand for secondhand clothes rises. One marketplace reported active buyers up 17%, to 1.47 million in a quarter. Again — inferior means demand moves opposite to income, not that the product is poor.

### Cross-price elasticity

The percentage change in the quantity of one good divided by the percentage change in the price of another. Here the **sign carries the meaning**: positive means **substitutes** (the other good gets more expensive and customers switch to yours), negative means **complements** (the other good gets more expensive and demand for yours falls with it).

The worked case: a cinema raises its ticket price from $15 to $18, a 20% increase, and popcorn sales fall 8%. The cross-price elasticity is −0.4 — negative, so tickets and popcorn are complements. That is a large part of why theatres keep ticket prices lower than you might expect: the ticket is what sells the popcorn.

The news example was fuel and vehicles. When petrol prices spike, demand for fuel-hungry vehicles falls — they are complements to fuel — and demand shifts to a substitute, electric vehicles, because electricity has become relatively cheaper. The class also showed estimated cross-price elasticities across four breakfast cereals: a positive figure between two of them means a price rise in one lifts demand for the other, which is what "substitutes" looks like in a real estimate. This is the number pricing teams and antitrust authorities use to decide which products actually compete.

## Part 3 — Elasticity and revenue

### Which way does revenue move?

Total revenue is price times quantity, and a price change pushes those two in opposite directions. Which effect wins is exactly the elastic-inelastic split. **Where demand is elastic, cutting the price raises total revenue**, because the quantity gain outweighs the lower price. **Where demand is inelastic, cutting the price lowers total revenue.** Revenue peaks where the elasticity is −1.

The class ran that logic backwards on a live case. In early 2026, under a deal with the White House, Novo Nordisk cut the US self-pay price of Ozempic and Wegovy from $499 to $349 a month, and then guided 2026 sales revenue **down** by 5 to 13%. A price cut that reduces revenue tells you demand was **inelastic** at that price. And profit fell on both ends: revenue down, while the extra units sold pushed production costs up. The market agreed — the shares fell about 18% on the warning.

The counter-case was fast food: McDonald's and its US franchisees cut combo-meal prices by about 15% relative to buying the items separately and extended the value menu, a move that only makes sense if demand is elastic enough for the volume to more than compensate. A detail worth remembering is that McDonald's set aside roughly $35 million to compensate franchisees hit by the lower prices — because locations face different demand elasticities, and the incentives had to be aligned.

### Marginal revenue, and why it sits below the price

Marginal revenue is the change in total revenue from selling one more unit. For a firm facing a downward-sloping demand curve, it is **below** the price, and the reason is worth stating carefully: to sell that extra unit you have to lower the price — and you lower it on **every** unit you sell, not just the marginal one. The revenue you give up on all the other units has to be subtracted from the price of the new one.

The class showed the mechanics for a linear demand curve, where marginal revenue starts at the same point on the price axis as demand but falls twice as fast. It also connects back to the elasticity story: where marginal revenue is negative you are on the inelastic portion, which is precisely where you should be raising your price.

## Part 4 — Where the numbers come from

### Experiments

The cleanest way to estimate a demand curve is to vary the price on purpose and watch what happens — A/B testing. The class used online retail as the illustration: a free price-tracking site has followed Amazon listings since 2008, and a single listing plotted over about five months moved almost daily, swinging between roughly $35 and $84 for the same item. That is not one experiment; it is continuous experimentation on price.

### Regression

The other route is to fit a demand equation to data — quantity as a function of price, with a negative coefficient on price. The class worked through an airline pricing experiment. The estimate put the intercept at about 479 seats and the price coefficient at about −1.64, meaning each one-unit price cut buys about 1.64 more seats sold, and it explained roughly 60% of the variation in quantity.

Then the payoff, using the tools from earlier in the module. At a price of 140, the fitted demand gives about 249 seats, and the point elasticity works out to about **−0.92** — inelastic. Which means, by the rule the class had already established, the airline should **raise** the price.

Adding more variables improved the picture: a competitor's price entered with a positive coefficient, as you expect for a substitute, and an income measure entered positive too, marking the product as a normal good. Both are elasticities in disguise, out of the same regression.

### Correlation is not causation

The module closed on the caution. Prices in ordinary transaction data are not set at random — they move when demand moves, when competitors move, when a promotion runs. So a correlation between price and quantity in historical sales data can reflect something else entirely: the classic problem of an **omitted variable** driving both. The class showed some deliberately absurd spurious correlations to make the point stick, and used it to explain why deliberately **randomizing** prices is worth the trouble. If you set the price at random, nothing else systematically moves with it, and the relationship you estimate is the demand curve rather than an artefact.

## The one takeaway

Module 2 started with a fact everybody already knows — customers buy less when the price goes up — and turned it into a number. Elasticity is unit-free, so it travels across products and markets. Its sign tells you what kind of good you are selling and which products compete with yours. Its size tells you whether a price increase will grow or shrink your revenue, and whether you are pricing in a place that leaves money on the table. And the last part of the module was about earning the right to trust that number: run the experiment where you can, and be sceptical of a correlation you did not create on purpose.
