## Slide 4 — Recap of Module 2

Let's take sixty seconds to remind ourselves where Module 2 left us.

We covered the law of demand – the idea that, holding everything else constant,
when price falls, quantity demanded rises. We put numbers on how strongly
customers respond, using elasticities – responsiveness to a product's own price,
to income, and to competitors' prices. We connected demand to revenue, and saw
how own-price elasticity drives total revenue and how to find marginal revenue.
And we talked about how firms actually estimate demand in practice, through
experiments and regression analysis.

So the revenue side of the business is now in hand. Tonight we crack open the
cost side. Once we have both, profit-maximization in Module 4 falls out almost
mechanically.

## Slide 6 — The Big Picture of Module 3

Let me give you the big-picture frame for the night, because it's easy to lose

Almost every important decision you've ever made as an executive – what to

There are two parallel trees here. On the left is Production. The production

## Slide 8 — Part 1: Production

Alright, let's start with Part 1 – Production.

The core question for the next stretch is simple to state: how does output depend
on inputs? We'll build that up in two steps. First, short-run hiring decisions –
when your plant is fixed and labor is the only thing you can change, how many
people should you hire? Then, the long run – when you can choose everything from
scratch, what's the right mix of capital and labor? Let's begin with the
production function itself.

---

## Slide 9 — The Production Function

Here's the production function in its most basic form: output, Q, is a function
of the inputs a firm uses – capital, which we call K, labor, which we call L,
and anything else, like raw materials or energy.

A word on capital, because it's broader than people expect. Capital isn't just
buildings. It's the machinery, the software, the intellectual property, the AI
systems – anything you've already invested in that keeps producing for you.

The callout at the bottom captures the big idea in one sentence: a production
function transforms inputs into outputs, and the more efficiently it does that,
the higher your productivity. That word – productivity – is going to come up
again and again, and everything else in this module is built on this one
expression.

---

## Slide 10 — Short Run vs. Long Run: A Critical Distinction

This next distinction is one of the most important in the whole course, so let's
be careful with it.

In the short run, some inputs are fixed – we call them fixed factors – and you
simply cannot change them within your planning horizon. The long run is a horizon
long enough that every input becomes variable.

The two photos make it concrete. On the left is Rivian's assembly plant in
Normal, Illinois. In any given quarter, the building and the robot line are
fixed – capital is fixed. Rivian can add shifts and hire more workers, but it
can't conjure up a second factory overnight. On the right is an ophthalmologist.
Highly trained specialists are also a fixed factor in the short run – a hospital
can't produce another surgeon on a month's notice.

Both examples teach the same lesson: in the short run, you optimize around the
factors you can't change. And that's exactly what we'll do next, starting with
the marginal product of labor.
