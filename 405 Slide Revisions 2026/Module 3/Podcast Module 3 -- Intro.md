## What Module 3 will cover (preview — build intuition, but no worked examples or numbers)

Module 3 tackles two basic questions every business faces: how do we turn inputs into output (**Production**), and what does it all cost (**Costs**)? The point is never theory for its own sake — it's how these ideas drive real decisions about hiring, investment, pricing, and growth. Here's the intuition to walk in with.

On the **production** side, a company turns inputs — people and machines — into output. A key fact you'll build on is **diminishing returns**: if you keep adding workers to the same fixed set of machines and floor space, each extra worker still adds output — the total keeps rising — just a little less than the one before, because they're all sharing the same equipment. (Diminishing returns means output grows more slowly, not that it falls; production would only drop under *negative* marginal product, which we won't realistically hit.) This sets up a sharp distinction between the **short run** — where you're stuck with the factory you already have and can only adjust the workforce — and the **long run**, where you get to build the right-sized plant and choose the whole mix of people and machines. In the short run the central question is hiring, and the answer is beautifully simple in spirit: keep adding people as long as one more brings in more than they cost, and stop when those two balance. In the long run a different idea takes over, the "bang for the buck" rule: put each dollar where it buys the most extra output, balancing people against machines by value per dollar rather than by raw productivity. It's worth being clear which world you're in, because the two lead to genuinely different decisions. A running electric-vehicle example, a striking story from centuries ago, and a very current one from the AI talent wars will make all of this concrete in class.

On the **costs** side, the class sorts out which costs should actually drive a decision — and a few that quietly mislead even sharp managers. A **sunk cost** is money already spent that you can't get back, and the counterintuitive rule is to ignore it completely going forward: only future costs and benefits should count. Its close cousin is **opportunity cost** — the true cost of using money or people on one thing is the value of the best alternative you gave up. You'll also separate **marginal cost**, the cost of making just one more unit, from **average cost**, and see why good decisions ride on the marginal one — confusing the two leads to bad calls on everything from gym packages to loans. A famously over-budget Hollywood film (where the problem was the runaway cost of *making* it, not ticket sales) and some very large recent bets in tech will bring the sunk-cost idea to life.

Finally, two ideas about size. **Economies of scale** means that as a firm gets bigger, its cost per unit can actually fall — big fixed investments get spread over more units, and specialization kicks in — which is why a large airline or a company like Amazon can be cheaper per unit than a small rival. But bigger isn't always better: past some point, coordination and bureaucracy pile up and cost per unit starts climbing again, which is **diseconomies of scale**. **Economies of scope** is the cousin idea — making several related products together can be cheaper than making each one alone, because they share things like research, a brand, or a supply chain.

Listen for the one habit of mind running underneath all of it: weighing the benefit of doing a little more against the cost of doing a little more, and pushing to the point where they meet. Once the class puts that idea front and center, you'll start seeing it behind nearly every business decision you make. Come ready to dig in.
