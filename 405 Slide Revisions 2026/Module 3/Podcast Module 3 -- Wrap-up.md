## Part 1 — Production: turning inputs into output

### The production function and the two time horizons

A **production function** is just the recipe that turns inputs into output. Write it as Q = f(K, L): output Q depends on capital K (factories, machines, robots, software) and labor L (workers), plus materials and energy. The more output you get from a given set of inputs, the more productive you are.

The crucial split is **short run versus long run**. In the short run, capital is fixed — you're stuck with the plant and the machines you already have, and the only lever you can pull is labor. In the long run, everything is flexible — you get to choose the plant size and the whole mix of capital and labor from scratch. These two situations lead to genuinely different decisions, so the class treated them separately.

### Diminishing marginal product — the workhorse idea

The **marginal product of labor** is the extra output you get from one more worker. The central fact of short-run production is that it **diminishes**: as you keep adding workers to the same fixed capital, each additional worker adds less than the one before, because they're all sharing the same machines and floor space.

The teaching example was the electric-vehicle maker **Rivian**. Hold the number of robots fixed and keep adding workers, and each new worker adds a little less output than the one before — the tenth worker on a line contributes more than the thousandth, because they're all sharing the same machines and the same floor space. Same pattern, over and over.

A wonderful historical illustration ran the idea in reverse. Before about 1800, economies were agricultural: land was the fixed input, labor was variable. When the **Black Death** killed roughly half of Europe's population in 1348, the survivors were suddenly working the same land with far fewer hands. With labor now scarce relative to land, the marginal product of each remaining worker jumped — and wages of survivors rose sharply. That's diminishing marginal product working backwards, documented six centuries before economists gave it a name.

### The short-run hiring rule

How many workers should a firm hire? The intuition is refreshingly simple: keep hiring as long as one more worker brings in at least as much extra revenue as you pay them, and stop when the two are about equal. The benefit of a worker is the revenue they add; the cost is their wage. That's the MB = MC idea in work clothes.

Back to Rivian, and remember this was the short run — the plant and the robots were fixed, so hiring was the only way to make more cars. Each additional worker produces some extra cars, and each car brings in roughly $45,000 after materials, so that worker generates some extra revenue for the week. As long as that extra revenue comfortably clears the weekly wage, Rivian should keep hiring. But because each worker adds a little less than the last, the extra revenue keeps shrinking — and once it falls to about the level of the wage, you've found the right headcount. There's no need to grind through the arithmetic; the logic is just "does the next person pay for themselves?"

### Wage searchers: when hiring moves the price

The simple rule assumes the wage is fixed. But a firm that is large relative to its labor market bids the wage up when it hires a lot — a hospital hiring twenty specialized surgeons, or an AI lab hiring a hundred senior researchers in a year. Economists call such a firm a **wage searcher**, and its true marginal cost of labor is higher than the sticker wage, because a raise for the new hire usually spreads to everyone.

The vivid case was the **AI talent war**. Imagine Anthropic poaching a star researcher from a rival for a $5 million salary, while it already employs two stars at $3.5 million each. The moment the newcomer arrives at $5 million, the other two demand the same. So the *full* marginal cost of that third researcher isn't $5 million — it's $5 million plus two raises of $1.5 million, which is **$8 million**. Big buyers of scarce talent move the market price, and smart executives budget for it. Meta ran into exactly this dynamic paying up to retain AI researchers.

### The long run: the best mix of capital and labor

In the long run both inputs are flexible, so the question shifted from "how many workers" to "what mix of robots and workers." The rule is the **bang-for-the-buck rule**: spend each additional dollar wherever it buys the most extra output. Equalize the marginal product per dollar across inputs.

Rivian's Georgia plant made it concrete — and note this was the long run, so Rivian got to choose both how many robots and how many workers to use. A robot produces more cars than a single worker does, so automation looks tempting at first glance. But a robot costs something like $20,000 a week, against roughly $1,200 for a worker. Once you ask what each extra *dollar* buys — rather than what each extra machine or person produces — the worker turns out to deliver more output per dollar. So the smarter mix there was more workers and fewer robots. And when relative prices change, the mix tilts: put a heavy tax on robots and you lean toward labor; let wages spike and you lean toward automation.

The same logic runs your grocery cart. **Tide** detergent comes in a small bottle (29 loads for $17, about 1.7 loads per dollar) and a large one (81 loads for $35, about 2.3 loads per dollar). More cleaning per dollar from the big bottle, so bang-for-the-buck says buy large — the identical rule Rivian uses to choose between robots and workers.

## Part 2 — Costs: which costs actually matter

### Three kinds of cost, and the one executives get wrong

Costs come in three buckets. **Variable costs** move with output (materials, hourly labor, shipping) — always weigh them. **Fixed costs** don't move with output (rent, insurance, past R&D) — they matter mainly for big long-run choices about entering, exiting, or changing capacity. And **sunk costs** — money already spent that can't be recovered — should be ignored entirely in any forward-looking decision. The five-word version: *sunk costs are not costs.* This is where even very smart people go wrong.

**Waterworld**, the 1995 Kevin Costner film, was the classic. The studio knew it was over budget and likely to disappoint, so why release it? Because the release decision depends only on whether the extra revenue from releasing beats the extra cost of releasing. Over time, more and more got sunk into production, but the money still needed to finish and release always stayed well below what the film could earn. So at every stage, releasing beat shelving — even at the very end, when the total spend including everything already sunk looked like a loss on paper. The sunk money was gone; only the cost still to come mattered.

The modern version was **Meta's Reality Labs**. Meta poured on the order of $70 billion (the Wall Street Journal puts it north of $77 billion) into virtual and augmented reality since 2020, with thin adoption and a payoff that kept slipping. In 2026 it redirected a big chunk of that spending to AI. Classic sunk-cost discipline: the tens of billions already spent are irrelevant to where the next dollar should go.

**Apple's canceled car** added the twin idea of **opportunity cost**. Apple killed Project Titan in 2024 after roughly a decade and about $10 billion. That $10 billion was sunk — not a reason to continue, and not a reason to quit. The real reason to stop was opportunity cost: the capital and especially the ~2,000 engineers had a higher-value use in Apple's AI work. Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best use of the same dollars and people, and it's the number that should actually drive a "keep or kill" call.

### Marginal cost — the number that drives output decisions

**Marginal cost** is the cost of producing one more unit, and it's usually the decision-relevant cost (average cost is more of an accounting summary). Two quick real-world traps show why you must compute it at the margin.

A boutique fitness studio, **Burn60**, sells a 10-class pack at $10 a class ($100 total) and a 20-class pack at $8 a class ($160 total). What does each *extra* session cost if you already have the 10-pack? Not the $8 sticker. Going from the 10-pack to the 20-pack costs $60 more for 10 more sessions, so the marginal cost is $6 per session — even cheaper than the sticker, because the bigger pack also re-prices the first ten.

Finance had the same shape. A $100,000 loan at 5% costs $5,000 a year; a $110,000 loan at 6% costs $6,600. The headline 6% is an *average*. The marginal cost of the extra $10,000 is the extra interest over the extra principal: $1,600 on $10,000, which is a **16%** marginal rate. Always ask what the extra dollars actually cost, not what the average rate is.

### Cost curves and the sweet spot

Real cost functions bend. Rivian's Georgia plant carried a big weekly fixed cost, plus a cost per vehicle that crept up as the plant got busier, so marginal cost *rose* at high output. Expressed per vehicle, average total cost is **U-shaped**: it falls at first as that fixed cost spreads over more cars, bottoms out at a sweet-spot volume, then climbs again as marginal cost takes over. Only past a certain output does average cost dip below the sale price and the plant start making money on average. And a handy rule: average cost is at its lowest exactly where marginal cost crosses it.

A great "estimate it yourself" case was the **iPhone** — the class walked through roughly what one costs Apple to make. Add up the per-unit pieces, materials plus assembly labor plus distribution, and you land around $480 a phone in variable cost. Fold in the fixed costs, mostly R&D and marketing, spread across the model's entire lifetime sales, and the all-in average cost comes to something under $600. Against a retail price of about $1,200, that leaves a comfortable gap for overhead and profit. The real lesson was the method: break a messy question into buckets you can each estimate.

### Economies of scale and scope

In the long run, average cost can fall, stay flat, or rise as a firm grows — **economies of scale**, constant returns, or **diseconomies of scale**. Falling average cost (bigger is cheaper per unit) shows up where huge fixed costs spread over enormous volume — utilities, chip fabs. It comes from specialization and the division of labor (Ford's assembly line beats one craftsman), from indivisibilities (you can't build half a network or half an R&D lab, so the fixed cost is the same whether you serve one million customers or a hundred million), and from geometry (in a ship, a fuselage, or a pipeline, useful volume grows faster than the surface area you pay for).

**Aviation** showed it in one number. A regional jet like the Embraer E175 (76 seats) has a cost per available seat-mile around 15 cents; a wide-body like the Boeing 787-9 (~290 seats) is down near 9 cents — roughly 40% cheaper per seat-mile. Fuel, pilots, and landing fees scale with the airframe, not the seat count, so spreading them over far more seats drives the unit cost down.

But bigger eventually bites back — **diseconomies of scale**. Coordination, communication, and control get harder faster than the firm grows; reporting chains lengthen, incentives misalign, and support functions (HR, legal, compliance) balloon. Past some size, these frictions outweigh the savings and average cost turns back up.

**Economies of scope** are the cousin: producing two or more related products together is cheaper than separately, because they share expensive capabilities — engineering, brand, supply chain, customer data. Airbus shares engineering DNA across the giant A380 and the small A318; Apple shares chips and an operating system across iPhone, iPad, and Mac.

**Amazon** was the textbook "both at once." Scale: AWS is a fixed-cost-heavy business spread across millions of customers, the fulfilment network gets cheaper per package as it densifies, and size brings supplier bargaining power. Scope: Prime bundles shipping, video, music, and grocery, and customer data created in retail feeds advertising and AWS. A quick Shark Tank-style case made it tangible: a beverage whose sales rise from 135,000 to 300,000 cans while average cost falls from $1.30 to $1.10 a can is showing economies of scale in miniature.

## The one takeaway

Strip away the vocabulary and every decision in Module 3 was the same move: think at the margin. Hire the next worker if the revenue they add beats their wage. Add the next robot only if the last dollar buys more output there than on labor. Finish the movie, or cut the car, based on the money still to come — never the money already gone. Price the next unit, the next loan dollar, the next seat-mile at its marginal cost, not its average. Every hiring, pricing, and capacity call was, at bottom, comparing marginal benefit with marginal cost — MB = MC.
