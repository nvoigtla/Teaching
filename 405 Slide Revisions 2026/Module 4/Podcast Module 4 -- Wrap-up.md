## What Module 4 covered

### 1. Where a firm sits: the four market structures

The module opened by sorting markets along a spectrum, using three features: how
many firms there are, how similar their products are, and how hard it is to
enter. More firms, more alike the products, easier the entry – the more
competitive the market.

At one end was **perfect competition**, where a firm's slice of the market is so
small that nothing it does moves the price. At the other end was **monopoly**, a
single seller, like a city utility. In between sat **monopolistic competition** –
petrol stations, restaurants – where products are differentiated enough to give
each firm a downward-sloping demand curve but substitutable enough that
competition stays fierce; and **oligopoly**, a handful of firms who each have to
think about how rivals will respond. Firms at the competitive end are **price
takers**; firms with market power are **price searchers**. Module 4 was about the
price takers.

### 2. Perfect competition, from the inside

Three conditions had to hold: many firms, an identical product, and no barriers
to entry. The consequence is that each firm faces a **flat demand curve at the
market price**. Charge a fraction more and you sell nothing, because buyers have
thousands of identical alternatives. There is no reason to charge less either,
since you can already sell as much as you like at the going price.

The German farm protests made this concrete. Germany cut its farm subsidies in
2024, and some farmers stood to lose up to ten thousand euros a year. A farmer in
that position cannot pass the cost on – the price of his crop is set by the
market, not by him – so it comes straight out of his margin. That is why the
response was tractors on the streets rather than higher prices at the farm gate.
The political route was the only one left.

Worth remembering why this matters to a manager: almost no business is a textbook
price taker, but many face something close to it in at least one market – a
commodity input, a spot market. And much of what firms actually do is work to
make their market look *less* like this one.

### 3. How much to produce

Every profit maximiser produces where marginal revenue equals marginal cost. The
useful thing about a price taker is that marginal revenue is just the market
price – every extra unit sells for the same amount – so the rule collapses to
**produce where marginal cost equals price**. Price is handed to you; quantity is
the only decision left.

Seen through totals rather than margins: total revenue is a straight line because
the price is constant, while total cost rises and then curves upward as
diminishing returns bite. Profit is the vertical gap between them, and it is
widest exactly where the two curves run parallel – another way of saying marginal
revenue equals marginal cost.

Enter the **Yi family**, who moved from California to rural Arkansas to grow
Korean cabbage and sell it to vendors in Dallas at whatever the market price
happened to be. Their fixed costs – land rent, equipment, and the salary they
gave up by farming rather than working elsewhere – came to sixty thousand dollars
for the season. Their variable costs were seeds, water, fertiliser and
pesticides, rising more than proportionally with output. At four hundred dollars
a ton they set marginal cost equal to price, found their tonnage, and made a
positive economic profit.

A second, more managerial lens gave the same answer: **average total cost**.
Divide total cost by quantity, compare it with the price, and you know at once
whether you are making money – the profit is a rectangle, price minus average
total cost by the number of tons. The class also paused on a real annual report
to make the point that accounting profit is not economic profit: the cost lines
leave out **opportunity costs**, like the salary the Yi family gave up.

### 4. Whether to produce at all – the short-run decision

Then the question that trips people up. If the firm is losing money, should it
stop?

Not necessarily, and the reason is the whole point of the section. In the **short
run** capital is fixed and the fixed costs are already committed – the land rent
and the equipment are owed whether or not a single cabbage is grown. So the fixed
costs are irrelevant to the decision. What matters is whether the revenue from
producing covers the costs that producing actually adds: the **variable** costs.

Hence the short-run rule: **continue to produce if price is at least average
variable cost; stop production if price is below it.** This is a different test
from the profit test. A firm can be making a loss and still be right to keep
producing.

The class ran the Yi family straight into it. The United States started importing
large quantities of cabbage from China and the market price fell from four
hundred dollars a ton to two hundred and ten. Same rule, new price – and the
optimal tonnage falls. At that quantity the family now makes a **loss** of a
little under fifty-three thousand dollars.

So should they stop? No. Stopping production means losing the whole sixty
thousand of fixed costs, because that money is owed either way. Producing means
losing about fifty-three thousand. Every ton sold contributes something towards
the land and equipment they are paying for regardless. Keep producing through the
season, and revisit in the long run. That comparison – fifty-three thousand
against sixty thousand – is the cleanest way to see why fixed costs drop out of
the short-run decision.

Then a case that ran the other way. A **coffee bean producer**, already at its
profit-maximising output. Because it is a price taker at its optimum, marginal
cost equals price, so the price was ten. But its average variable cost was
twelve. Every unit sold lost money on its variable cost alone, so producing made
the loss *bigger* than the fixed costs by themselves. The right answer was to
**stop production**, pay the fixed costs, and take that as the smaller loss.

The class then walked the three price cases through on the diagram: price above
average total cost, so the rectangle is profit; price between average variable
and average total cost, so it is a loss but production continues; and price below
average variable cost everywhere, so production stops. Doing all three again on a
realistic U-shaped cost function showed that nothing about the rule changed.

### 5. From one firm's costs to the market's supply

A neat result: because a price taker produces where marginal cost equals price,
tracing the quantity it would supply at each price just traces its marginal cost
curve. **The marginal cost curve is the firm's short-run supply curve.**

Then the class changed the costs. Suppose the Yi family switches to high-yield
seeds that cut the cost of every ton: the marginal cost curve shifts down, and at
the unchanged market price the optimum moves out. Lower costs meant *more
output*, not just more profit per ton.

The more interesting version was what happens when **every** farmer adopts them.
Market supply shifts out too, so the price falls. Each farmer still produces more
than before, but noticeably **less** than if she had been the only one with the
new seeds. In a competitive market, a cost saving everyone gets is largely passed
through to customers rather than kept as profit.

### 6. The long run: entry, exit, and zero profit

Two things changed. First, **capital can be adjusted** – the Yi family can decide
whether to rent land at all, and how much. All costs become variable, no fixed
costs remain, and the benchmark becomes **long-run average cost**. Second, **firms
can enter and leave**, which shifts market supply and moves the price.

The long-run rule: **stay in the industry if price is at least long-run average
cost; shut down and exit, or never enter, if it is not.** Losses cannot be
sustained once capital is adjustable, because there is nothing left to be
committed to.

Put entry and exit together and the market is pushed to a resting point. Price
above long-run average cost means economic profits, so new firms enter, supply
rises and the price falls. Price below it means firms exit, supply contracts and
the price rises. The process stops where **price equals long-run marginal cost
equals long-run average cost, and economic profit is zero.** Zero economic profit
does not mean nobody earns a living – it means nobody earns more than their next
best alternative.

The story that carried this was **Cecile Steele** of Ocean View, Delaware. In the
1920s she kept a small flock of chickens for eggs. In 1923 she ordered fifty
chicks and, by mistake, received five hundred. She kept them, raised them for
meat, and made a sizable profit. Word spread, and by 1928 hundreds of farmers in
the area were raising chickens for meat. That is entry in action, and precisely
why her extraordinary margins did not last.

The class then pushed the same toolbox somewhere less comfortable: the market for
**illegal drugs**, treating dealers as price takers. Go after the **dealers** and
you raise the cost of supplying, so supply shifts back. Quantity falls – but the
price rises, so surviving dealers earn more per unit, and those profits attract
entry, which in the long run drifts the market back towards where it started. Go
after the **users** instead and demand shifts back: now both quantity and price
fall, dealers make less, some exit, and the market settles with less consumed.
Which is why economists tend to favour the demand side here.

### 7. Market distortions: who wins, who loses, what disappears

The second half asked what happens when something pushes a market away from its
competitive equilibrium. Three tools. **Consumer surplus** is the gap between
what buyers would have been willing to pay and what they actually paid.
**Producer surplus** is the same gap on the selling side. **Deadweight loss** is
the part that simply disappears – transactions both sides would have been glad to
make, which the intervention prevents.

A **sales tax** was the first application. It drives a wedge between the price
buyers pay and the price sellers receive. Buyers lose some surplus, sellers lose
some, the government collects revenue – and a slice is lost outright, because
some trades no longer happen. Only part of what buyers and sellers give up
reappears as revenue.

Then the point that surprises almost everybody: **tax incidence**. Who physically
hands the money to the government makes no difference to who bears the tax.
Collect it from the buyer or from the seller and the burden splits the same way.
What determines the split is **elasticity** – the side that responds *less* to
price ends up paying more. If supply is perfectly elastic, consumers bear all of
it.

The **minimum wage** came next, a price floor set above the market wage. Workers
who keep their jobs are paid more and gain. But firms hire fewer workers, so some
people who would have worked do not, and they lose. The gap between labour
supplied and labour demanded at the floor is unemployment, and part of what is
lost is deadweight loss.

The evidence, though, was genuinely mixed. Theory predicts employment falls. What
studies tend to find is that employment itself does not drop much while **hours
worked** do – the Seattle study is the well-known example. For experienced
workers the decline in hours was moderate enough that total income still rose;
for low-skilled and inexperienced workers it was large enough that income fell,
and new entrants found it harder to land a first job.

**Rent control** was the mirror image – a price ceiling below the market price.
Renters who hold a controlled flat gain, but landlords offer fewer flats, so
quantity supplied falls and a shortage opens up. The class made this vivid with a
one-bedroom flat in Santa Monica, a five-minute walk from the beach, and asked
everyone to guess the rent. It is rent-controlled, and the city's own database
showed a maximum allowable rent far below what neighbouring flats fetch.

Price is also not the only thing a landlord can adjust. When the rent cannot
rise, maintenance can slide, tenants can be bought out, and – illegally – tenants
can be harassed into leaving.

Then the empirical test, which is the part worth remembering. Rent control in
**Cambridge, Massachusetts** ended suddenly in 1995, letting researchers compare
previously controlled units against never-controlled units nearby. Rents rose
sharply at the decontrolled units, as you would expect. But they also rose at
units that had **never** been controlled, and investment went up at both. The
spillover was the surprise: rent control had been holding down the value of the
whole neighbourhood, not just the controlled flats.

And a recent case: **Argentina** repealed its rent-control law at the end of
2023. Since then many more flats have been listed and rents are lower in real
terms – though those are figures reported in the press rather than the result of
a study, so hold them more loosely than the Cambridge findings. And not everyone
gained: sitting tenants lost their protection.

### 8. Externalities

The last section dealt with costs and benefits that land on someone who was never
part of the transaction. A **negative externality** is a cost imposed on an
outsider – air pollution from a coal-fired power plant, or the roughly one dollar
fifty a gallon of external cost from burning petrol. A **positive externality** is
a benefit conferred on an outsider, like a neighbour's well-kept front yard.

The problem is that the person deciding faces only their own cost. Drivers weigh
what a gallon costs *them*, so the market settles where demand meets private
marginal cost. Add the cost everyone else bears and you get **social marginal
cost**, and the efficient quantity is smaller than the market quantity. The
market does too much of the harmful thing – not through bad intentions, but
because a real cost is in nobody's calculation.

Two families of fix. **Price mechanisms** – tax the activity that imposes the
cost, subsidise the one that confers the benefit – change what the decision-maker
faces and let the market find the quantity. A tax set equal to the external cost,
about a dollar fifty a gallon for petrol, moves the market to the efficient
quantity: consumers pay more, producers receive less, and the wedge is the tax.
**Quantity mechanisms** – quotas, caps, mandates – set the quantity directly: a
limit on the tonnes of sulphur dioxide a plant may emit, or childhood vaccination
as a condition of school entry.

The module closed on **Santa Monica airport**, where the arithmetic gets
uncomfortable. Every flight imposes noise on the neighbourhood that the pilot
does not pay for. Add that noise cost to the pilots' own cost of flying, and
social marginal cost lies above what anybody is willing to pay at *every*
quantity. No positive number of flights passes the test. The efficient answer is
zero flights – a corner solution – which is to say: close the airport. And that
is broadly the direction Santa Monica went.

### Pulling it together

In the short run a price taker produces where marginal cost meets the price and
keeps producing as long as the price covers average variable cost, because the
fixed costs are owed either way so stopping production saves nothing on them. In
the long run capital becomes adjustable, the test becomes price against long-run
average cost, and entry and exit compete economic profit away to zero.

And once we intervene in a market – or once a cost lands on someone outside the
transaction – consumer surplus, producer surplus and deadweight loss are how we
work out who gained, who lost, and what was given up for nothing at all.
