**NOTES:** Here we start thinking about a firm’s competitive environment or market structure. We categorize market structure using three characteristics:

1. Number of firms: in general, when there are more companies in the market, the market is more competitive.
2. Whether the consumer cares which company made the good: are the goods identical or can the consumer distinguish who made the good? In general, if goods are closer to identical, the market is more competitive.

**NOTES:** Extent of market power. The spectrum runs from price taker (today’s topic) to price searchers with increasing degrees of market power (subject of subsequent sessions): 
 
A perfectly competitive firm has no market power; it has such a small percentage of the market that it can exert no effect on price. 
 
A monopolistically competitive firm, such as gas stations and restaurants, offers a differentiated product and thus faces a downward sloping demand curve – offerings of other firms are not perfect substitutes. There is still intense competition because the products can substitute for each other so the elasticity of demand facing each firm tends to be high. Free entry drives long-run profits to zero.
 
Oligopolistic markets are cases of few firms. For example, a duopoly is a special case of an oligopoly with only 2 firms. Interesting strategic issues arise here as you need to think hard about your rivals’ response to your firm’s behavior. Entry may be limited.
 
Monopolistic markets are markets with only one seller, such as utilities in most cities (legal monopoly) or Microsoft (quasi-monopoly, due to demand-side economies of scale, i.e. network effects).

**NOTES:** Characteristics of a price taker market. Today we focus on perfect competition. For a market to be perfectly competitive, three conditions must be satisfied:

There are many firms. Thus, the impact of any one firm on the market equilibrium is negligible. 
All firms produce an identical product.
There are no barriers to entry.

The implications of these conditions is that firms in perfectly competitive markets are price takers, i.e. they do not have discretion over the price to charge. In the case of price takers, they face perfectly elastic demand (horizontal demand curve), there is no product differentiation, and they cannot deviate in price. Examples include Indian rice producers, sellers of currency, etc. If the firm chooses a price above the market price, the firm will lose all sales. Since the firm can sell as much as it would like at the market price, it has no incentive to sell at a quantity below the market price.

Why study perfect competition? There are 3 main reasons:
 
You need to understand how perfectly competitive markets around you function. For instance, the bond market, the currency markets, commodity markets, labor markets, etc, where price taking behavior is frequent. 
You might have to act in perfectly competitive markets on occasion.
By understanding perfect competition in depth you can come up with ways to make the market in which you operate look more like an imperfect competition market. This is what businesses do – differentiate, raise barriers to entry, gain market power.

**NOTES:** To measure profit, we subtract total cost from total revenue.

Profit = TR – TC
We can rewrite this as:
Profit = P*Q – ATC*Q
Profit = (P – ATC)*Q

This formula determines whether the firm has positive, zero, or negative profit.

Positive profit: In this figure, the firm’s total revenue is the area of the rectangle with height P and width Q*. The total cost is the area of the rectangle with the height ATC* and the width Q*. Because total revenue exceeds total costs at Q*, the firm is earning positive profits. This equation tells us that profit is positive when P>ATC at the profit maximizing quantity Q*. 

Zero profit: Here P=ATC (=MC). We know that MC intersects ATC at its minimum. 

Negative profit: P<ATC

**NOTES:** A study on the end of rent control in Cambridge, MA finds results consistent with economic theory. When the price control is removed, the price of previously controlled units goes up, i.e. adjusts to the free market equilibrium price. Investment in these units also rises, consistent with landlords’ improvement of their units in order to command a higher price. Interestingly, the study finds that price and investment in nearby never-controlled units also rise. This is likely due to a positive spillover from the improvement in the quality of the controlled units. 

For more, see the original study: https://economics.mit.edu/files/9774
