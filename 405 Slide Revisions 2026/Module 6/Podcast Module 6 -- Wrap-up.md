## What Module 6 covered

### 1. The dilemma of simple pricing

The module opened with the trap that every one-price business is in. Charge a
high price and you collect a lot from each buyer, but you lose everyone whose
willingness to pay sits below it. Charge a low price and you sell to almost
everyone, but you hand a large surplus to the customers who would happily have
paid far more. One price cannot do both jobs.

The class made that concrete with a thought experiment, and it matters that it
is one. Netflix sells a flat monthly subscription. The question on the slide was
what would happen *if* it used simple pricing instead, meaning one simple price
per movie. On a stylised demand curve, and taking the cost of serving one more
stream as zero, the best simple price per movie works out at two dollars, six
films get watched, and the month brings in twelve dollars. That is not what
Netflix earns. It is the benchmark the rest of
the module beats, and the reason for building it was to see what a single price
throws away.

What that twelve dollars leaves behind comes in two parts, and the class drew
both on the same diagram. Above the price sits the consumer surplus, the value
the customer walks away with because she would have paid more than two dollars
and was not asked to. To the right sits the unexploited market, the viewers who
would have watched at some lower price and were shut out. Neither is a cost of
doing business. The slide labels the pair "wasted profits", and that is the
right way to read them: both are money the SELLER fails to reach. That picture,
a revenue rectangle with a triangle on either side of it, is the motivation for
everything that follows, and everything that follows is measured the same way,
in profit to the firm.

### 2. What complex pricing requires

Complex pricing, or non-uniform pricing, means charging different prices for
essentially the same product. Module 6 was clear that it takes three things at
once.

First, **market power** – a downward-sloping demand curve, a price searcher
rather than a price taker. A firm with no pricing power has no pricing strategy
to have, which is why the whole module is about price searchers.

Second, **customers who genuinely differ**, either individually or in groups.

Third, and this is the one that kills strategies in practice, **the ability to
prevent resale and arbitrage**. Spangler Candy makes Dum-Dums, and resellers were
buying them cheaply at Sam's Club, listing them on Amazon at a markup, and
undercutting Spangler's own channel while doing it. Those sellers manufacture
nothing and improve nothing. They move identical candy from a cheap channel to an
expensive one and keep the spread, and they can only do it because the same
product is available at two prices. Where resale is that easy the customers come
out ahead and the firm is pushed back to selling everything at the one low price.

### 3. Three degrees, and what they are really about

The three degrees of price discrimination were presented as three answers to one
question: **how much does the firm know about the person in front of it?**

- **First degree**, perfect price discrimination: the firm knows each individual
  customer's willingness to pay.
- **Third degree**, segment pricing: the firm cannot see individuals but can sort
  people into groups that differ on average.
- **Second degree**, indirect price discrimination: the firm knows nothing about
  who is who, so it designs a set of choices and lets customers sort themselves.

Everything in the first half of the module was one of those three.

### 4. First degree: the hypothetical ideal

Perfect price discrimination means a different price for every buyer, set at what
that buyer will pay. It needs customers who differ, a firm that knows how they
differ, and no resale. Do it and the firm captures the whole area under the
demand curve. Both triangles from the opening diagram are gone: there is no
surplus left uncollected, and no sale lost to anyone willing to pay above cost.
It is the upper bound on what any pricing strategy can earn, which is why the
module starts from it.

The class was careful here: this remains largely hypothetical. Knowing each
person's valuation is hard, arbitrage undermines it, and some of it runs into
legal limits. But firms keep getting closer, and the class put the question of
what that trend means to the room rather than answering it from the front –
so leave it open here too.

The live demonstration was ride-hailing – ten people, same destination, same
moment, half on one app and half on the other, reading out their quoted price.
Worth saying plainly that the claims in this area are press reports rather than
settled findings.

### 5. Third degree: segment pricing

This is the one firms actually use: split customers into groups with different
price sensitivities and charge each group its own price. It needs groups that
really do differ on average, a way to identify who belongs to which, and no resale
between them. The rule is the familiar one applied twice – set marginal revenue
equal to marginal cost **in each market separately** – and the direction is always
the same: the lower price goes to the more elastic segment.

The examples ran through the everyday and the surprising:

- **Seniors at the cinema, and students.** The discount goes to the group the
  firm judges more price-sensitive, which is the rule stated on the slide rather
  than anything specific to seniors or to students. What the firm does need is
  a way to check the status at the door, which is the "can you identify the
  group" condition doing its work.
- **Discounts for locals**, where a resident pays less than a visitor for the
  same admission.
- **Timing.** A new film costs more in its opening weeks than the same film in the
  same seat two months later. What is priced is impatience.
- **Orbitz and the Mac users.** The travel site found that people browsing on a
  Mac spent more per night on hotels, and started sorting pricier options to the
  top for them. It never asked which segment you were in, it inferred it from a
  signal you did not know you were sending – which is also why this kind of
  segmentation draws a backlash once customers find out.
- **The same arthritis drug for humans and for dogs**, priced very differently.

Two worked cases anchored the section. The **Lufthansa puzzle** came back from
week 1: the connecting itinerary from Los Angeles to Istanbul via Frankfurt cost
*less* than the direct Los Angeles to Frankfurt flight, on the same seat on the
same aircraft plus a second leg on top. On cost grounds that is nonsense. On
segment-pricing grounds it is obvious: the passenger who must be in Frankfurt has
no good substitute and a steep demand curve, while the one heading to Istanbul
has a dozen routings and a flat one. The less elastic market gets the higher
price.

And **BMW** put numbers on why splitting pays. Two separate prices, 60 thousand
dollars in the US and 70 thousand in Germany, earned about 1.7 billion. The best
single price for both together, 65 thousand, earned about 1.6 billion. The gap is
roughly 6 percent, and it always runs the same way: one price applied to two
different demand curves can never beat giving each curve its own.

Segment pricing only survives while the segments stay apart. Textbooks bought
cheaply in India and resold in the States show how fast arbitrage closes a gap,
and consumer anger and the Robinson-Patman Act add constraints of their own. That
is why firms so often take the indirect route instead.

### 6. Second degree: versioning and coupons

Here the firm cannot identify the groups at all, so instead of pricing the
*customer* it prices *versions of the product* and lets people choose. It still
needs market power and customers who differ. What it no longer needs is any way
of telling who is who: the sorting is incentive-compatible, and customers who pay
the high price do so willingly.

The design rule is worth memorizing: the premium version has to deliver genuine
value to the high-willingness-to-pay customer while costing the firm little more
to make, and the cheap version has to be **genuinely worse**, or the high-value
customers will cheerfully take it. The slide puts it as keeping the
low-quality option inferior enough that premium customers are not tempted,
which is what incentive-compatibility means here and why no one has to worry
about resale between the versions.

- **Internet service sold in speed tiers**, FedEx and UPS ground against
  same-day, and the three cabins on an aircraft. In each the versions cost
  nearly the same to supply and sell for very different prices.
- **Costco's two membership tiers**, the standard card and the executive one,
  with the customer picking. The money is made on the memberships and the goods
  sold at the cheapest price possible, which is why the shelf prices look the
  way they do.
- **Three Barbie dolls** – a farmer, a robotics engineer, and an Yves Saint
  Laurent doll – at very different prices. The plastic is nearly identical.
  What differs is who is buying, and Mattel never had to ask. (The class guesses
  the three prices before they are revealed, so they stay off this episode.)
- **The major US airlines against Southwest and Spirit.** They neither matched
  the fare nor held the price. They added a stripped version of the same seat:
  no free carry-on bag, no advance seat assignment, no miles. The cheap fare
  competes at the low end while the full fare keeps the high end intact, which
  is the whole design rule in one move.
- **Wendy's**, the cautionary tale. Varying prices by time of day drew immediate
  political and customer backlash. What is technically available is not always
  available in practice.

**Coupons** do the same job with time as the sorting device. Clipping them takes
effort, and the people willing to spend it are the price-sensitive ones, so the
discount reaches exactly them and everyone else pays full price, without a
question being asked. Contrast haggling, the historical version: slow, and not
incentive-compatible, since everybody has a reason to misrepresent what s/he
would pay.

### 7. Changing the question: one customer, many units

Halfway through, Module 6 set the whole first half aside. Suppose every customer
has the *same* demand curve, but each buys several units. The surplus is still
there to be captured, but the reason is different now: what varies is the value
of the second unit against the first. Three tools, and which one fits depends
almost entirely on marginal cost.

**Flat fee, when marginal cost is zero.** Back to Netflix, and now to what it
actually does. With no cost of serving another stream the customer watches
until the next film is worth nothing to her, so the firm can charge for the
whole area under the demand curve instead of a rectangle inside it. On the same
stylised numbers that comes to twenty-four dollars a month, exactly double the
twelve the simple price per movie managed. Same customer, same demand, twice the take,
and she watches twelve films instead of six. So the thought experiment the
module opened with has its answer: the flat monthly fee is not a billing
convenience, it is the pricing strategy. The real plans then show the flat fee
is itself versioned – Standard with Ads, Standard and Premium, at very different
prices for what costs Netflix almost the same to supply.

The class also showed what goes wrong when marginal cost is *not* zero. American
Airlines sold an unlimited lifetime first-class pass in the 1980s at more than
350,000 dollars. Very few people bought it, and those who did flew constantly,
which is exactly the prediction: the marginal trip is free to the customer and
still costs the airline real money.

**ClassPass** was the modern version, and it does two things at once. You buy a
bundle of credits per month rather than a class, and more credits per month costs
less per credit – that is volume pricing. And a popular studio or a six o'clock
slot costs more credits than an off-peak one – that is segment pricing by time.

### 8. Two-part tariffs, when serving a unit costs something

Once marginal cost is positive the fix is two prices, one decision made twice. The
**usage fee** is set by efficiency: put it at marginal cost and the customer stops
exactly where the value of the last unit equals what it costs to provide. The
**flat fee** is set by extraction: take the consumer surplus left above it.

**Zipcar** is the clean case. Marginal cost is fifty cents a mile, so charge fifty
cents a mile and the member drives a hundred miles, the right amount. The surplus
above that line is the membership, fifty dollars. Charge no usage fee and members
would drive far past the point where the miles are worth anything.

The **zoo exercise** put it against the alternatives. Marginal cost is eight
dollars a visit, so set the per-visit charge there and the average visitor comes
four times. The surplus left above that line is sixty-four dollars, and that
becomes the membership. The per-visit charge collects thirty-two dollars, exactly
the variable cost of the four visits, so the zoo keeps the sixty-four. Against the
two alternatives:

- **One simple price**: marginal revenue equal to marginal cost gives two visits
  at twenty-four dollars, and thirty-two dollars per customer. The two-part
  tariff doubled it – and did so by serving the customer *more*, not less.
- **A pure flat fee**: a hundred dollars for unlimited entry, and she comes all
  five times because each visit is now free to her. Those five visits cost the
  zoo forty dollars, leaving sixty. The fifth visit is the whole problem: she
  values it at nothing, and it still costs eight dollars to serve. The usage fee
  is what prevents that, and here it was worth four dollars.

**Costco** turned out to be both halves of the module at once: two membership
tiers are versioning, and membership plus shopping is a two-part tariff with the
shelf prices close to cost. Video game consoles and their games, and Amazon Prime and
the purchases, share the structure.

### 9. Block pricing

The last tool. Block pricing lowers the price as the *same customer* buys more,
and the difference from segment pricing is the thing to hold on to: the discount
is tied to the **quantity one person takes**, not to **who that person is**, so
the firm needs to know nothing about the customer at all.

Walmart's photo prints were the worked example, with each larger block cheaper per
print and customers walking themselves up to the largest one. The everyday version
is ice cream: four dollars for one scoop, seven for two, eight fifty for three, so
the second scoop costs three dollars and the third one fifty. Each block sits
under the same declining demand curve, and the customer walks down it handing over
most of the surplus on the way.

### 10. The decision tree

Module 6 closed by putting everything on one page, as four questions asked in
order. Does the firm have market power? No, and you are a price taker with no
pricing strategy to have. Can it prevent resale? No, and you are back to one
simple price. Do customers have different demand curves? No, and what is left is
the advanced set: flat fee, two-part tariff, block pricing. Can the firm identify
customers directly? Yes, and it is direct price discrimination, individually in
the first-degree ideal and by group in third-degree segment pricing. No, and it
is indirect, second-degree pricing, with customers sorting themselves.

### Pulling it together

One price leaves value on the table in two directions at once: surplus handed to
the customers who would have paid more, and sales lost to the customers who would
have paid less. Every strategy in Module 6 was a route to some of it. If you can
identify your customers you price them directly; if you cannot, you build
versions and let them sort themselves. And when one customer buys many units the
tool follows marginal cost: a flat fee when serving another unit is free, a
two-part tariff once it is not.

Two constraints ran underneath all of it. Resale destroys any of these strategies
if the firm cannot prevent it, and customers, as Wendy's discovered, will
sometimes refuse a scheme the economics says should work.
