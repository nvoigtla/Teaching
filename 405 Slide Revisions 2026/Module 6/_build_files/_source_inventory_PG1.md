**NOTES:** When an individual can buy several units of an item, the seller can think about a volume pricing strategy. This is the case of continuous choice as opposed to binary choice (buy/don’t buy). Diminishing marginal personal value comes into play – the value of each subsequent unit falls. 
 
We will talk about 3 kinds of volume pricing; we will see examples of each - they all amount to the same thing:
— Fixed fee + access price
— All or nothing pricing: bundle.
— Quantity discounts: sliding scale. 
Examples of volume pricing:
— Rockreation: fixed monthly fee, unlimited access. Also, price differences with SLC. Is it price discrimination (probably, no – may reflect cost differentials, especially rent)
— Health Club: Annual fee
— Car Wash: Fixed fee - unlimited washes 
— Netflix: Monthly fee – “unlimited” rentals

Analytics will help to clarify all this here. We still abstract from cost and set MC=0. The optimal price is where Q=10 because the slope of MR is twice the slope of D and we are setting MR=0. Under simple pricing, there is unexploited volume equal to the lower triangle ($100 of value) and the consumer retains a surplus of $100. The company ends up with revenues of $200. There is some opportunity to do some business here.

**NOTES:** Ask in class if this is a price searcher or price taker

The dilemma of pricing is that if you charge a high price to capture a lot of value, you exclude many from wanting to buy the product, namely the hordes of those with valuations for the product that are lower than price. The alternative is to go with volume, but then you have to charge a low price and you give away a lot of consumer surplus, leave a lot of surplus on the table which you do not capture. There is a dilemma. Today we will see how to escape the dilemma through price discrimination.

**NOTES:** We reintroduce segment pricing, a concept already introduced in week 2 when we discussed Mercedes’s optimal pricing in the US and Germany, based on different elasticities.
 
At the car wash, senior citizens get a discount. This is price discrimination based on age. Why do firms select different prices for different segments? We talked about incentives to discriminate when different market segments exhibit different demand elasticities. You want to equate marginal revenue across segments, assuming marginal cost is equal across segments. The implication of this is that you want to charge a lower price for the more elastic segment. 
 
So why are senior citizens more price sensitive?
 
Going to the car wash is a time consuming activity. It can take 40 minutes, sometimes more for an upscale treatment. Senior citizens can afford to drive around longer for a better deal. Opportunity cost of time. They have lower income, so they are more price sensitive The opportunity cost of time for a senior person is much lower than say for a professional. A professional is concerned about the value of time. Senior citizens don’t care so much about the time component of the cost of the car wash, so they are more sensitive to price.

**NOTES:** Impediments to segment pricing.
There are legal impediments: at the federal level in the US, the Robinson Patman Act of 1936 bars price discrimination that results in anticompetitive outcomes. There are also state statutes on this, so instance in California laundry services are barred from charging different prices to men and women. What defense would you use to justify such price differentials? Cost differentials.
 
Another problem is parallel sales from low price segments to high price segments. You need to keep your segments separate. In many instances people would not bother, but you can think of cases where it would make sense to arbitrage across segments – pharmaceuticals for instance. AIDS drugs in Africa example.
 
The main impediment is revelation of valuations: how does the firm find out what the valuations of the different segments are? The problem is that price discrimination per se is not incentive compatible: low elasticity customers have an incentive to misrepresent their demand elasticity so that it is perceived as high and they can get a lower price. For seniors at the carwash, it is hard to conceal their age. But in general it is hard to figure out who is price sensitive.
