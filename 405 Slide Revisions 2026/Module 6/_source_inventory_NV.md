The dilemma of pricing is that if you charge a high price to capture a lot of value, you exclude many from wanting to buy the product, namely the hordes of those with valuations for the product that are lower than price. **NOTES:** We reintroduce segment pricing, a concept already introduced in week 2 when we discussed Mercedes’s optimal pricing in the US and Germany, based on different elasticities.
 
At the car wash, senior citizens get a discount. This is price discrimination based on age. Why do firms select different prices for different segments? We talked about incentives to discriminate when different market segments exhibit different demand elasticities. You want to equate marginal revenue across segments, assuming marginal cost is equal across segments. The implication of this is that you want to charge a lower price for the more elastic segment. 
 
So why are senior citizens more price sensitive?
 
Going to the car wash is a time consuming activity. It can take 40 minutes, sometimes more for an upscale treatment. Senior citizens can afford to drive around longer for a better deal. Opportunity cost of time. They have lower income, so they are more price sensitive The opportunity cost of time for a senior person is much lower than say for a professional. A professional is concerned about the value of time. Senior citizens don’t care so much about the time component of the cost of the car wash, so they are more sensitive to price.

**NOTES:** An example of Second Degree Price Discrimination: Versioning
 
We will go in greater depth into one form of second degree price discrimination which is versioning. Versioning is incentive compatible and accomplishes everything that price discrimination accomplishes. Consumers who buy at a high price do so willingly. The key to understand this is that the version priced at a premium has to have features that generate some value to the consumer, but the cost differential to the company is minor relative to the price differential (otherwise there is no real discrimination on price if the surplus captured is the same across segments).
 
The perfect example is Microsoft: offers Windows Professional and Windows Home Edition. Windows Pro has more features than Home edition but they are otherwise close substitutes. The marginal cost of producing each is identical (and approximately zero) – in fact it may cost the company something to actually differentiate the products. Sometimes it costs nothing to make something better but you don’t want to do it: why doesn’t Microsoft offer the full version of Windows to all their customers since the MC of doing so is zero? Because they would then join the markets, and forego the benefits of price discrimination. You add features to the price insensitive segment so you can charge them more.
