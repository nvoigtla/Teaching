**Notes:**
> Oligopoly means markets with few sellers, but more than one (which is monopoly). How few? What is the distinction between oligopoly and monopolistic competition?
> There are barriers to entry in oligopolistic markets, keeping the number of competitors low and creating the potential for long term abnormal profits.
> There is strategic interdependence in oligopoly: under monopolistic competition, each firm ignores price changes by other firms because each is so small their price behavior does not affect your behavior. Under oligopoly, each has an incentive to respond to others’ deviations.

## Slide 18 (slide18.xml)
**Title:** Two models of oligopoly competition
**Pictures:** Picture 9 → ../media/image26.jpg; Picture 7 → ../media/image27.png
**Body:**
- Cournot (1801-1877)
- Cournot Model: Competition in Quantity
- Bertrand (1822–1900)
- Bertrand Model: Competition in Price
- 18
**Notes:**
> Oligopoly: Cournot versus Bertrand
> In what follows we will assume no collusion: competition rather than cooperation between firms. There are a lot of models of oligopoly – as many as assumptions you are willing to make on rivals’ responses. You will come back to these issues in your strategy class, for now we give the foundations that will be used in strategy.
> Two oligopoly models – the two most famous ones
> Cournot assumed quantity competition: each firm sets quantity taking as fixed the quantity set by the other. The assumption is that the other firm will continue to produce the same quantity whatever I do. As we will see shortly price does not go to the competitive level but settles between the monopoly and perfect competition price.
> Bertrand (in his review of the Cournot book) proposed an alternative – that competition occurs on prices, not quantity. That is, the rival’s price is held fixed when setting price. The idea is that in response to my price cut, others don’t match. The equilibrium is where this assumption turns out to be correct (as before the assumption is false for all except the equilibrium point). What happens? As we will see shortly, equilibrium ends up equal to perfect competition.
> Note: Both types of competition are in commodities, so lend themselves to the perfect competition model. That’s why Cournot had quantity competition in mind – just like price takers who adjust their supply and let the market determine the price.

**Notes:**
> Cournot assumed quantity competition: each firm sets quantity taking as fixed the quantity set by the other. The assumption is that the other firm will continue to produce the same quantity whatever I do. [Of course most of the time this assumption will prove false, but not at the equilibrium]. The residual demand curve is just the amount of demand left over when subtracting the other firm’s fixed supply from market demand. As we will see shortly price does not go to the competitive level but settles between the monopoly and perfect competition price.
> This can be solved in two steps:
> Step 1: Go from the market-level demand to the individual-level demand by “assuming” QB=50: P=100-(QA+50)=50-QA
> Step 2: Once you have the individual-level demand, just find the profit-maximizing quantity as usual (i.e., MR=MC). For that, multiply P=50-QA by QA to get to TRA, and then take derivative with respect to QA to get to MRA=50-2QA. Finally, find the point where MRA=MC: 50-2QA=10  QA=20.

> Now: Don’t say they move sequentially. It’s just “contemplating”
> Now, the last step is to figure out the Cournot equilibrium. This is given by the “intersection” of the two reaction functions. The intuition is that we want a pair (QA*,QB*) so that A wants to respond to QB* with QA*, and B wants to respond to QA* by picking QB*.
> We start from the reaction function for A found in the previous slide:
> QA=45-0.5QB (1)
> Then we can repeat the analysis for firm B (in this game, since everything is symmetric, we should find the mirror image of it):
> QB=45-0.5QA (2)
> Last, we need to find a pair (QA*,QB*) that satisfies the two equations. This is a system of two equations and two unknowns, so it is straightforward to solve. One way to solve is to just take the formula for QA in (1) and replace for the QA in (2):
> QB=45-0.5 (45-0.5QB)= 22.5 + 0.25 QB
>  QB-0.25 QB= 22.5  0.75 QB= 22.5  QB=22.5/0.75=30
> For QA, since the game is symmetric, it should be true that QA=30 as well. If you want to verify this, just replace QB=30 back in (1):
> QA=45-0.5*(30)=45-15=30
> This figure is just a graphical representation of the intersections of the two reaction functions. Notice that, because these are two lines with different slopes, they must intersect in one and only one point.

**Notes:**
> Bertrand assumed that firms choose a price and stand ready to meet all demand at that price.
> Each firm sets a price that maximizes profits holding constant the price set by the other firm. Contrast this with the Cournot case where the game is played on quantities.
> What is the outcome of Bertrand competition?
> The only equilibrium is P1 = P2 = MC. If, say P2 >= P1 > MC, P2 will undercut by a small amount, so there are not such equilibria.
> Proof: To prove this is an equilibrium, note that if you start with P1 = P2 = MC then neither of the firms can gain from increasing or dropping the price: if one increases the price, it will continue making the same profits (zero); and if one drops the price, then that firm will have lower profits (i.e., negative instead of zero profits). You can also prove that this is the only equilibrium. For that, just note that if you start with P2 >= P1 > MC, P2 will want to undercut the other firm by a small amount, so it cannot be an equilibrium. This is the situation represented by the figure (starting with P1=P2=$40 and then one of the firms dropping the price to $39).
> Bertrand with identical goods has a nice solution but the conditions that give rise to this outcome are rare in the real world. A market that was close was the online market for computer chips for consumers that like to build their own CPUs. Consumers bought the cheapest chip. Researchers found that prices were approx. marginal cost. An interesting wrinkle is that computer chip retailers attempted to differentiate their goods through obfuscation: either by advertising an inferior chip as a loss leader and then redirecting the consumer to their website or by offering product ad ons. In a Bertrand model with differentiated goods, there is the potential for the equilibrium price to be above marginal cost.

> Cournot assumed quantity competition: each firm sets quantity taking as fixed the quantity set by the other. The assumption is that the other firm will continue to produce the same quantity whatever I do. [Of course most of the time this assumption will prove false, but not at the equilibrium]. The residual demand curve is just the amount of demand left over when subtracting the other firm’s fixed supply from market demand. As we will see shortly price does not go to the competitive level but settles between the monopoly and perfect competition price.
> This can be solved in two steps:
> Step 1: Go from the market-level demand to the individual-level demand by “assuming” QB=50: P=100-(QA+50)=50-QA
> Step 2: Once you have the individual-level demand, just find the profit-maximizing quantity as usual (i.e., MR=MC). For that, multiply P=50-QA by QA to get to TRA, and then take derivative with respect to QA to get to MRA=50-2QA. Finally, find the point where MRA=MC: 50-2QA=10  QA=20.
