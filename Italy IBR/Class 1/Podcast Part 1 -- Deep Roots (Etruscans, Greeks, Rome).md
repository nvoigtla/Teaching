## 1a. Geography and the pre-Roman template
- **Slide anchors:** many cultures across the peninsula; Matera settled since
  ~10,000 BC; Valcamonica rock art; Etruscans from ~800 BC; Greeks in the south;
  ethnic patchwork ~400 BC; Romanization.
- **Beyond the slides:**
  - Geography sets the terms: a long peninsula, the Apennine spine, few great
    rivers, but enormous **coastline** — Italy is oriented toward the **sea and
    trade** and internally divided by mountains. Fragmentation is partly
    geographic destiny.
  - The **Etruscans** (Etruria — Tuscany/Lazio/Umbria): a confederation of
    independent cities (the "twelve peoples"), not a unified state — an early
    rehearsal of Italy's city-based pattern. Wealth from **metals** (iron from
    Elba, copper) and Mediterranean trade; skilled metalwork. They transmitted
    the alphabet (via the Greeks), engineering (drainage, the arch), and urban
    forms to Rome. Politically fragmented, they were absorbed by Rome by roughly
    300–100 BC.
  - **Magna Graecia:** Greek colonies from the 8th c. BC — Cumae, Neapolis
    (Naples), Taras (Taranto), Sybaris, Syracuse. They brought **vines and
    olives** (still core to the economy), **coinage**, and the polis. The south
    was, for a time, richer and more urban than the north — worth stressing,
    because the later North–South *reversal* is not primordial.
  - **Carthaginians/Phoenicians** in western Sicily and Sardinia: the
    Mediterranean was a contested trade world long before Rome.
- **Business-environment thread:** the peninsula's DNA is set early —
  **trade-oriented, maritime, city-based, fragmented** — and prosperity is
  already regional and shifting, not national.

## 1b. Rome — the first integrated market
- **Slide anchors:** founded 753 BC; kingdom then Republic (509 BC); checks and
  balances (magistrates, Senate, assemblies); Caesar; Augustus and the ~200-year
  Pax Romana; decline after 180 AD; split 395; fall of the West 476 (Odoacer).
- **Beyond the slides:**
  - **Republican institutions** matter for business history: Roman **private
    law** — property, contract, the legal person — is the ancestor of European
    commercial law (later codified as Justinian's *Corpus Juris Civilis*,
    529–534, rediscovered in the 11th–12th c. and taught at **Bologna**, the
    first university, ~1088).
  - Rome built the **first large integrated market** in the West: a common
    currency (the *denarius*), **roads** (the Via Appia from 312 BC), ports,
    aqueducts, and the Mediterranean as *Mare Nostrum* — effectively a customs
    and security union. Grain flowed from Sicily, North Africa, and Egypt to
    feed a metropolis of perhaps a million.
  - Economic organization: **latifundia** (large slave-worked estates),
    **publicani** (tax-farming companies — the *societates publicanorum*, among
    antiquity's closest things to joint-stock firms), and long-distance trade in
    wine, oil, grain, and luxuries.
  - **Why it declined** (nuance for the class): overextension and fiscal-military
    strain; the third-century crisis (civil wars, coin debasement, inflation —
    Diocletian's failed price edict of 301); the shift of gravity east
    (Constantinople, 330); and the binding of tenants to the land (the *coloni*,
    a step toward serfdom). **476 is a symbolic marker, not a sudden collapse.**
- **Business-environment thread:** Rome shows the payoff of **integration** (law
  + infrastructure + a single market + security) — and its collapse shows the
  cost of **dis-integration**. The next thousand years are, economically, the
  slow story of Italy losing and then partially rebuilding market integration at
  the level of the *city*.

**Go-deeper prompts:** Was Roman law Italy's most durable economic export? How
much of the later North–South gap is prefigured by the Republic-era pattern of
elite *latifundia* in the south?
