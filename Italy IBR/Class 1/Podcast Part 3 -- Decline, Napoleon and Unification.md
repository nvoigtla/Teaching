## 3a. The long decline, ~1500–1800
- **Slide anchors:** discovery of America shifts activity to north-western
  Europe → Italian city-states decline; the south occupied by Spain (heavy taxes
  for Spanish wars); Italy impoverished and falls behind.
- **Beyond the slides:**
  - **The Atlantic shift:** after 1492 (Columbus — a **Genoese** sailing for
    Spain) and da Gama's sea route to India (1498), world trade's center of
    gravity moved to the **Atlantic** powers — Portugal, Spain, the Dutch, then
    England. Italy's role as the Mediterranean **entrepôt** between East and West
    eroded — a textbook case of a leading region losing its locational rent to a
    geographic/technological shift.
  - **Deindustrialization and shocks:** Italian textiles lost markets to cheaper
    English and Dutch "new draperies"; the catastrophic **plague of 1630** (the
    backdrop of Manzoni's *I Promessi Sposi*) killed a large share of several
    northern cities. Urbanization and real wages stagnated.
  - **Foreign domination and the refeudalized south:** the **Spanish Habsburgs**
    ruled Milan, Naples, Sicily, and Sardinia through the 16th–early 18th c.,
    taxing heavily for their wars and **entrenching the southern barons and
    *latifundia*** — the south's landed, extractive structure hardened. After the
    **War of the Spanish Succession (1701–14)**, Austria took Milan and (for a
    time) Naples; the Bourbons later formed the **Kingdom of the Two Sicilies**.
    In the north, **Habsburg Lombardy** under Maria Theresa and Joseph II saw
    relatively **enlightened reforms** — again the north/south gap widens.
- **Business-environment thread:** Italy falls to the European **periphery**.
  Foreign rule plus the refeudalized south cements the **dual economy**; the
  north keeps more dynamism and better institutions.

## 3b. Napoleon — a natural experiment in institutions
- **Slide anchors:** Spain loses its grip after 1700; Napoleon conquers most of
  Italy (1796–1809); imposes administrative and legal reforms (**Code Civil**);
  a sovereign Italian state in the north-east. Then the copyright-and-operas
  paper: French conquest imposed **copyright** in Lombardy–Venetia in 1801,
  **raising the quantity (2.2×) and quality** of new operas.
- **Beyond the slides:**
  - Napoleon reorganized Italy: the **Cisalpine Republic (1797)** → **Italian
    Republic (1802)** → **Kingdom of Italy (1805)** with Napoleon as king and
    his stepson Eugène as viceroy; **Naples** under Murat. He imposed the **Code
    Napoléon** (uniform civil law — property, contract, equality before the law,
    attacks on feudal privilege), the **metric system**, meritocratic
    administration, suppression of guilds, and secularization of church lands —
    **planting modern legal-economic institutions**, especially in the north.
  - The **copyright natural experiment** (Giorcelli–Moser) is a gem for the
    class: because copyright reached Lombardy–Venetia (1801) but not the rest of
    Italy, and opera premieres are well documented, one can *measure* the causal
    effect of intellectual-property protection on creative output — and it's
    large. A clean illustration of **institutions shaping innovation and
    industry**.
- **Business-environment thread:** a rare moment when **modern institutions were
  imposed from outside** — and they took root more in the north than the south,
  reinforcing the divide. It's also the cleanest reminder that **rules
  (property, contract, IP) drive economic behavior** — a theme that runs
  straight into the present.

## 3c. The Risorgimento and unification (1815–1871)
- **Slide anchors:** Italy not unified for ~1,300 years; after Napoleon another
  patchwork; revolts aiming for a republic; **Garibaldi** (1834 uprising →
  death sentence → South America → returns 1848 → exile in New York); the
  winning 1860 strategy (expel Austria with French help — ceding **Nice and
  Savoy**; back a **strong monarchy** under **Piedmont/Victor Emmanuel II**);
  Garibaldi takes Sicily then Naples and hands power to the king. After
  unification: agrarian, illiterate (~3% spoke Italian — contested), poor
  infrastructure; huge North–South heterogeneity; the south's economy collapses
  after 1870 → the "Southern Question" and mass emigration.
- **Beyond the slides:**
  - The **Congress of Vienna (1815)** restored fragmentation under Austrian
    dominance. Three strands drove unification: **Mazzini's** republican
    nationalism (Young Italy), **Garibaldi's** military romanticism, and —
    decisively — **Cavour's** realpolitik. As Piedmont's PM, **Cavour**
    modernized the Piedmontese economy (railways, banking, free trade), then
    engineered a French alliance (Plombières, 1858) and war with Austria (1859),
    gaining Lombardy.
  - **Garibaldi's Expedition of the Thousand (1860)** — a thousand red-shirted
    volunteers conquering the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies — is the great story;
    he then subordinated his conquest to the monarchy. The **Kingdom of Italy was
    proclaimed 17 March 1861** (Victor Emmanuel II king); **Venetia** was added
    1866, **Rome** 1870 (capital from 1871).
  - **The economic reckoning — crucial for the class:**
    - Unification created a **national market**, but on **northern (Piedmontese)
      terms**: northern law, currency, tariffs, and taxes were extended
      nationwide.
    - The south was **not** a backward blank slate — the Bourbon kingdom had some
      industry (e.g., early ironworks; the Naples–Portici railway, 1839) — but it
      was **protected, state-dependent, and lacked a broad entrepreneurial middle
      class**. Exposed suddenly to northern and European competition (and hit by
      the **1887 protectionist tariff**, which helped northern industry and grain
      but hurt southern export agriculture), the southern economy **fell further
      behind**.
    - Result: brigandage and near-civil-war repression in the south (1861–65),
      then **mass emigration** — **millions** leaving between the 1870s and 1914,
      overwhelmingly from the south to the Americas — creating a structural
      **emigration-and-remittance economy** and a permanent *Questione
      meridionale*.
  - "L'Italia è fatta, ora bisogna fare gli italiani" — *Italy is made; now we
    must make the Italians* (attributed to Massimo d'Azeglio) — captures the core
    problem: a **state without a nation**, welded onto **two very different
    economies**.
- **Business-environment thread:** modern Italy is born **dualistic**. A single
  national market was created, but it **locked in and even widened** the
  North–South gap; emigration became the safety valve; and the **state** took on
  a central role that never really receded.
